# Basics of Accounting — Part 2_A

## Basic Accounting Terms

This lecture begins the next major section of the course: **Basic Accounting Terms**. These terms are essential because later topics such as the **Accounting Equation, Rules of Debit and Credit, Journal Entries, Ledger and Trial Balance** depend on understanding them clearly.

The sequence indicated in the lecture material is:

$$
\boxed{
\text{Basic Accounting Terms}
\rightarrow
\text{Accounting Principles}
\rightarrow
\text{Bases of Accounting}
\rightarrow
\text{Accounting Equation}
\rightarrow
\text{Rules of Debit and Credit}
}
$$

---

# 1. Capital

## Meaning

**Capital** refers to the amount invested by the proprietor/owner in a business.

It represents the owner's financial interest in the business.

Capital may also be described as:

* **Owner's Equity**
* **Net Worth**
* **Net Assets**

At a basic level:

$$
\boxed{\text{Capital}=\text{Assets}-\text{Liabilities}}
$$

Therefore, the lecture gives the basic accounting equation as:

$$
\boxed{\text{Assets}=\text{Liabilities}+\text{Capital}}
$$

### Example

Suppose the owner starts a business by investing ₹5,00,000.

Then:

* Assets increase by ₹5,00,000, typically as cash.
* Capital increases by ₹5,00,000.

Thus:

$$
\text{Assets} = ₹5,00,000
$$

$$
\text{Liabilities} = ₹0
$$

$$
\text{Capital} = ₹5,00,000
$$

and:

$$
₹5,00,000=₹0+₹5,00,000
$$

---

## 1.1 Capital as owner's claim

Capital is not simply "cash put into the business."

Once money is invested, the business owns the resulting resources, while the owner has a **claim/equity interest** in the net assets of the business.

For example, suppose an owner contributes ₹10 lakh and the business uses it to purchase machinery.

The owner's capital is still ₹10 lakh initially; it is the **form of the business's assets** that has changed from cash to machinery.

This distinction becomes important later when we study the accounting equation and double-entry system.

---

# 2. Drawings

## Meaning

**Drawings** are cash, goods or other business resources withdrawn by the owner for:

* personal use, or
* private purposes.

Examples include:

* Owner withdraws ₹20,000 cash for personal expenses.
* Owner takes inventory worth ₹5,000 home for personal use.
* Business pays a personal expense of the owner.

These withdrawals reduce the owner's equity in the business.

### Relationship with capital

Conceptually:

$$
\boxed{\text{Closing Capital}
=
\text{Opening Capital}
+
\text{Profit}
-
\text{Drawings}
+
\text{Additional Capital}}
$$

Thus, drawings are **not a business expense** in the usual sense.

They represent a withdrawal of the owner's interest from the business.

### Example

Opening capital:

$$
₹5,00,000
$$

Drawings:

$$
₹50,000
$$

Ignoring profit and additional capital:

$$
\text{Closing Capital}=₹5,00,000-₹50,000=₹4,50,000
$$

---

# 3. Liabilities

## Meaning

A **liability** represents an amount or obligation that the business owes to outsiders or otherwise has an obligation to settle.

Examples include:

* Loans
* Creditors / Trade payables
* Outstanding wages
* Outstanding expenses
* Other financial obligations

### Simple idea

$$
\boxed{\text{Liability = Amount/obligation owed by the business}}
$$

### Example

Suppose the business borrows ₹2,00,000 from a bank.

The business receives:

$$
₹2,00,000\text{ cash}
$$

but simultaneously has an obligation to repay the bank.

Therefore:

$$
\text{Liability}=₹2,00,000
$$

---

# 4. Classification of Liabilities

The lecture classifies liabilities in two ways:

### A. Based on whom they are payable to

1. Internal liabilities
2. External liabilities

### B. Based on when they are payable

1. Non-current liabilities
2. Current liabilities

Both classifications are useful, but they answer **different questions**.

---

# 5. Internal Liabilities

Internal liabilities are amounts attributable to the **proprietor/owner(s)** in the terminology used in the lecture.

In basic accounting treatment, the owner's claim is represented by **capital/equity**.

Thus, the lecture uses the term "internal liability" to refer to the amount the business owes/attributes to the proprietor or owners.

### Example

Owner's capital:

$$
₹1,00,000
$$

This represents the owner's claim on the business.

### Important modern terminology

In contemporary financial reporting, this is generally classified as **equity**, rather than being treated as a liability to external creditors.

So for conceptual clarity:

$$
\boxed{\text{Owner's claim = Equity}}
$$

while:

$$
\boxed{\text{External obligations = Liabilities}}
$$

---

# 6. External Liabilities

External liabilities are obligations that the business owes to **outsiders**.

Examples:

* Bank loans
* Trade creditors
* Outstanding expenses
* Other amounts payable

### Example

A business owes ₹75,000 to a supplier for goods purchased on credit.

Then:

$$
\text{Trade Payable} = ₹75,000
$$

This is an external obligation because the amount is payable to a party outside the ownership of the business.

---

# 7. Current and Non-Current Liabilities

Another important classification is based on **maturity/payment period**.

## 7.1 Current Liabilities

Current liabilities are obligations expected to be settled **within the relevant short-term operating cycle or generally within one year**, depending on the applicable accounting framework.

Examples:

* Trade payables
* Short-term borrowings
* Outstanding salaries
* Outstanding expenses
* Other short-term obligations

The lecture simplifies this as:

> Liabilities to be paid within 1 year.

---

## 7.2 Non-Current Liabilities

Non-current liabilities are obligations that are **not expected to be settled within the current short-term period**, commonly described in introductory material as those payable after more than one year.

Examples:

* Long-term bank loans
* Long-term borrowings
* Long-term debt obligations

The lecture simplifies this as:

> Liabilities to be paid after more than 1 year.

### Important note

The precise definition of current liability under formal accounting standards can involve additional conditions beyond simply "one year." For this introductory course, however, the one-year distinction is the useful basic rule to remember.

---

# 8. Case Study on Classification of Liabilities

The lecture provides the following case:

> Mr. X invests ₹10,000 in his business and takes a loan of ₹25,000 from SBI Bank for a period of 10 years. He then purchases goods worth ₹2,000 from Mr. Y on credit for 2 months.

Let's classify each amount.

### Transaction 1: Owner invests ₹10,000

This is the owner's contribution:

$$
\boxed{\text{Capital/Owner's Equity}=₹10,000}
$$

Under the terminology used in the lecture:

$$
\boxed{\text{Internal liability}=₹10,000}
$$

But conceptually in modern accounting:

$$
\boxed{\text{Equity}=₹10,000}
$$

---

### Transaction 2: Bank loan ₹25,000 for 10 years

The business owes ₹25,000 to the bank.

Therefore:

* It is an **external obligation**.
* Since it is due over 10 years, it is a **non-current liability**.

Hence:

$$
\boxed{\text{External liability}=₹25,000}
$$

$$
\boxed{\text{Non-current liability}=₹25,000}
$$

---

### Transaction 3: Credit purchase ₹2,000 from Mr. Y for 2 months

The business owes Mr. Y ₹2,000.

Therefore:

$$
\boxed{\text{External liability}=₹2,000}
$$

Since the amount is payable within 2 months:

$$
\boxed{\text{Current liability}=₹2,000}
$$

---

## Case Study Summary

| Item                       |  Amount | Classification                                            |
| -------------------------- | ------: | --------------------------------------------------------- |
| Owner's investment         | ₹10,000 | Capital / Equity; lecture terminology: internal liability |
| SBI Bank loan              | ₹25,000 | External + Non-current liability                          |
| Credit purchase from Mr. Y |  ₹2,000 | External + Current liability                              |

### Important insight

The same liability can simultaneously have **two classifications**:

> **External + Current**

or:

> **External + Non-current**

because the two classification systems are based on different dimensions.

---

# 9. Business Transaction vs Event

This is an important conceptual distinction introduced in the lecture.

---

## 9.1 Business Transaction

A **business transaction** is an economic activity of the business that **changes its financial position** and can be expressed in **monetary terms**.

Thus, a business transaction generally has:

1. A financial effect on the business, and
2. A measurable monetary value.

### Examples

* Purchase of goods
* Sale of goods
* Purchase of machinery
* Payment of salary
* Receipt of cash
* Borrowing from a bank

---

## 9.2 Event

An **event** refers to the **result or consequence of a transaction**.

The lecture illustrates this using a purchase-and-sale example.

Suppose:

* Goods are purchased for ₹2 lakh.
* They are sold for ₹2.5 lakh.

### Transactions

The following are business transactions:

1. Purchase of goods for ₹2 lakh
2. Sale of goods for ₹2.5 lakh

### Result / Event

The difference is:

$$
₹2.5\text{ lakh}-₹2\text{ lakh}
=₹0.5\text{ lakh}
$$

Therefore:

$$
\boxed{\text{Profit}=₹50,000}
$$

The lecture describes this profit as the **event/result arising from the transactions**.

---

## 9.3 Conceptual understanding

A useful way to understand the example is:

$$
\text{Business Transactions}
\rightarrow
\text{Changes in accounts}
\rightarrow
\text{Financial result}
$$

So:

* **Transactions** are economic activities.
* **Events/results** may arise from those activities.

### Important refinement

In broader accounting terminology, **"event" can have a wider meaning** than the narrow definition given in the lecture. An event may include an economic occurrence that affects the financial statements even when it is not a conventional exchange transaction.

For this course, remember the distinction emphasized in the lecture, while keeping this broader meaning in mind.

---

# 10. Assets

## Meaning

An **asset** is a valuable economic resource controlled by a business as a result of past events, from which future economic benefits are expected.

The lecture gives a simpler formulation:

> Assets are valuable resources owned by businesses that are acquired at a measurable monetary cost.

Examples given include:

* Cash
* Land
* Furniture

Other common examples include:

* Buildings
* Machinery
* Inventory
* Trade receivables
* Vehicles
* Computers

---

# 11. Important Characteristics of Assets

A useful conceptual understanding of an asset includes the following:

### 1. It provides economic value

The resource should have the capacity to provide future economic benefits.

### 2. It is controlled by the business

The business must have control over the resource.

### 3. It arises from a past event or transaction

For example, a machine may have been purchased in the past.

### 4. It can be appropriately recognised and measured

Accounting requires that assets satisfy the applicable recognition and measurement requirements.

---

# 12. Classification of Assets

The lecture divides assets into three broad categories:

1. Non-current assets
2. Current assets
3. Fictitious/Nominal assets

It also introduces another classification of non-current assets:

* Tangible assets
* Intangible assets

Let's examine each.

---

# 13. Non-Current Assets

## Meaning

Non-current assets are assets that are:

* Held for continued use in the business, particularly for producing goods or rendering services; and
* **Not intended for resale in the ordinary course of business**.

Examples given:

* Land
* Buildings
* Furniture

Other examples:

* Plant and machinery
* Office equipment
* Vehicles
* Computers used in operations

### Basic idea

$$
\boxed{
\text{Non-current asset}
=
\text{Long-term resource used in business}
}
$$

rather than inventory held primarily for sale.

---

## Example

A furniture manufacturer has a machine used to manufacture furniture.

The machine is a **non-current asset** because it is used to produce goods.

But the furniture manufactured for sale is generally **inventory**, a current asset.

Thus, classification depends partly on **how the resource is used by the business**.

---

# 14. Tangible Assets

A **tangible asset** has a **physical existence**.

Examples:

* Land
* Buildings
* Furniture
* Machinery
* Vehicles
* Computers

The important characteristic is:

$$
\boxed{\text{Tangible} \Rightarrow \text{Physical form}}
$$

### Example

A computer used by an office is tangible because it has a physical form.

---

# 15. Intangible Assets

An **intangible asset** does not have a physical form but may still provide economic benefits.

Examples given:

* Patents
* Goodwill

Other common examples can include certain:

* Copyrights
* Trademarks
* Software rights/licences

The key characteristic is:

$$
\boxed{\text{Intangible} \Rightarrow \text{No physical substance}}
$$

### Example

A patent has no physical shape like machinery does, but it can provide economic benefits to the business through legal rights.

---

# 16. Tangible vs Intangible Assets

| Basis              | Tangible Assets                      | Intangible Assets                               |
| ------------------ | ------------------------------------ | ----------------------------------------------- |
| Physical existence | Yes                                  | No physical substance                           |
| Examples           | Land, building, furniture, machinery | Patent, goodwill, certain copyrights/trademarks |
| Nature             | Physical resources                   | Rights/benefits without physical form           |

### Easy memory trick

**Tangible = touchable**

**Intangible = cannot be physically touched**

---

# 17. Current Assets

The lecture also refers to current assets as:

* **Floating assets**
* **Circulating assets**

These are generally assets that are:

* held for sale, or
* expected to be converted into cash/consumed within the short term, commonly within one year in introductory classification.

Examples:

* Debtors / Trade receivables
* Stock / Inventory
* Bills receivable
* Cash
* Bank balances
* Short-term investments in appropriate classifications

### Core idea

Current assets are generally associated with the **normal operating cycle and short-term liquidity** of the business.

---

## 17.1 Why are they called circulating assets?

Because they continuously move through the operating cycle.

For a trading business, for example:

$$
\text{Cash}
\rightarrow
\text{Inventory}
\rightarrow
\text{Sales}
\rightarrow
\text{Receivables}
\rightarrow
\text{Cash}
$$

Thus, the resources **circulate through the business**.

This is why the older term "circulating assets" is often used in traditional accounting teaching.

---

# 18. Example: Current vs Non-Current Asset

Suppose a company has:

* ₹10 lakh of machinery used for manufacturing
* ₹3 lakh of inventory held for sale
* ₹2 lakh receivable from customers

Then:

| Item              | Classification    |
| ----------------- | ----------------- |
| Machinery         | Non-current asset |
| Inventory         | Current asset     |
| Trade receivables | Current asset     |

The important issue is not simply how expensive the item is; it is also **how and for what purpose it is held**.

---

# 19. Fictitious / Nominal Assets

The lecture introduces **fictitious assets** as items that are neither tangible nor intangible assets in the normal sense but represent:

> **losses or expenses that are yet to be written off.**

Examples given include:

* Debit balance of Profit & Loss Account
* Deferred revenue expenditure

### Important modern accounting clarification

The term **"fictitious asset" is a traditional accounting term**.

Such items generally **do not meet the modern conceptual definition of an asset**, because they do not necessarily represent a controlled resource from which future economic benefits are expected.

Therefore, when studying from modern financial-reporting standards, it is better to think of "fictitious assets" as **unamortised/deferred amounts or losses appearing on the asset side under older/traditional presentation**, rather than genuine economic assets.

---

# 20. Why the Name "Fictitious Asset"?

They are called "fictitious" because they may appear in traditional balance-sheet presentation similar to assets, but they do not represent ordinary resources such as:

* cash,
* machinery,
* inventory,
* buildings.

For example, suppose a business incurs an amount that is not immediately charged fully to the profit and loss account under an older accounting treatment.

The unamortised amount may historically be shown on the balance sheet.

It is therefore described as a "fictitious asset."

Again:

$$
\boxed{
\text{Fictitious asset} \neq \text{genuine economic asset}
}
$$

---

# 21. Overall Classification of Assets

A useful hierarchy is:

$$
\boxed{\text{Assets}}
$$

### I. Non-Current Assets

Resources held for long-term use in the business.

Further classification:

$$
\boxed{
\text{Tangible}
\quad\text{and}\quad
\text{Intangible}
}
$$

### II. Current Assets

Resources expected to be sold, consumed, or converted into cash in the short term.

### III. Fictitious Assets

Traditional term for certain deferred/unwritten-off losses or expenses; not genuine assets under modern conceptual accounting.

---

# 22. Assets vs Liabilities

These two terms are fundamental.

### Asset

> What the business **owns or controls / has as an economic resource**.

### Liability

> What the business **owes or is obligated to settle**.

Example:

A company buys machinery for ₹5 lakh by taking a bank loan.

Immediately:

**Asset:**

$$
\text{Machinery}=₹5,00,000
$$

**Liability:**

$$
\text{Bank Loan}=₹5,00,000
$$

So:

$$
\text{Assets}=\text{Liabilities}+\text{Equity}
$$

and, ignoring other items:

$$
₹5,00,000=₹5,00,000+₹0
$$

---

# 23. Core Relationship Between Capital, Assets and Liabilities

The lecture's basic equation is:

$$
\boxed{
\text{Assets}=\text{Liabilities}+\text{Capital}
}
$$

Rearranging:

$$
\boxed{
\text{Capital}=\text{Assets}-\text{Liabilities}
}
$$

This equation will become the central foundation for the next lecture topics.

### Interpretation

Everything the business has obtained financially can broadly be financed by:

1. **Owners' funds** → Capital/Equity
2. **Outsiders' funds** → Liabilities

Hence:

$$
\boxed{
\text{Resources of business}
=
\text{Owners' claim}
+
\text{Outsiders' claims}
}
$$

---

# 24. Important Relationships to Remember

### Capital

$$
\boxed{\text{Capital}= \text{Owner's Equity}}
$$

### Drawings

$$
\boxed{\text{Drawings reduce owner's equity}}
$$

### Net worth

$$
\boxed{\text{Net Worth}=\text{Assets}-\text{Liabilities}}
$$

### Accounting equation

$$
\boxed{\text{Assets}=\text{Liabilities}+\text{Capital}}
$$

### Profit and capital

Profit generally increases owner's equity:

$$
\boxed{\text{Profit} \uparrow \Rightarrow \text{Equity} \uparrow}
$$

### Loss and capital

Loss generally decreases owner's equity:

$$
\boxed{\text{Loss} \uparrow \Rightarrow \text{Equity} \downarrow}
$$

### Drawings

$$
\boxed{\text{Drawings} \uparrow \Rightarrow \text{Equity} \downarrow}
$$

---

# 25. One Integrated Example

Consider the following transactions:

### Step 1: Owner invests ₹5,00,000

$$
\text{Cash} +₹5,00,000
$$

$$
\text{Capital} +₹5,00,000
$$

Equation:

$$
₹5,00,000=₹0+₹5,00,000
$$

---

### Step 2: Business takes a long-term bank loan of ₹2,00,000

$$
\text{Cash} +₹2,00,000
$$

$$
\text{Loan Liability} +₹2,00,000
$$

Now:

$$
\text{Assets}=₹7,00,000
$$

$$
\text{Liabilities}=₹2,00,000
$$

$$
\text{Capital}=₹5,00,000
$$

Therefore:

$$
₹7,00,000=₹2,00,000+₹5,00,000
$$

---

### Step 3: Purchase goods worth ₹1,00,000 on credit

The business receives inventory:

$$
\text{Inventory} +₹1,00,000
$$

and incurs a payable:

$$
\text{Trade Payable} +₹1,00,000
$$

Notice that total assets and total liabilities both increase by ₹1,00,000.

---

### Step 4: Owner withdraws ₹20,000 cash

$$
\text{Cash} -₹20,000
$$

$$
\text{Drawings} = ₹20,000
$$

Owner's equity therefore falls by ₹20,000.

This simple example connects:

$$
\boxed{
\text{Capital + Liabilities + Assets + Drawings}
}
$$

and sets up the accounting equation that will be developed further.

---

# 26. Important Terminology Cautions

Some terms in introductory/traditional accounting material are commonly used differently in modern financial reporting.

### "Internal liability"

Traditional texts may use this to describe the owner's claim.

For modern conceptual clarity:

$$
\boxed{\text{Owner's claim = Equity, not external liability}}
$$

### "Fictitious asset"

This is a traditional term and should **not** be interpreted as a genuine asset under modern accounting concepts.

### "Assets owned"

A more precise modern idea is that an asset is a **resource controlled by the entity**, rather than merely something legally "owned."

These distinctions will be useful as your course moves from traditional bookkeeping terminology toward formal financial analysis.

## Core takeaway

The most important ideas from **Part 2_A** are:

$$
\boxed{\text{Assets}=\text{Liabilities}+\text{Capital}}
$$

A business's resources (**assets**) are financed by the claims of **outsiders (liabilities)** and **owners (capital/equity)**. **Drawings reduce the owner's equity**, liabilities can be classified by whom they are owed and by their maturity, and assets can be classified according to their use, liquidity and physical nature.

# Basics of Accounting — Part 2_B

## Basic Accounting Terms — Continued

Part 2_A introduced **capital, drawings, liabilities and assets**. This section continues with another set of fundamental terms that will be used repeatedly throughout accounting:

$$
\boxed{
\text{Expenditure, Expense, Income, Revenue, Profit, Gain, Loss}
}
$$

and then moves to **trade-related terminology, bills, debtors, creditors, bad debts, insolvency, discount, stock and inventory**.

These distinctions are especially important because many of these words sound similar in ordinary language but have **different meanings in accounting**.

---

# 1. Expenditure

## Meaning

**Expenditure** refers to a disbursement of cash, transfer of another property, or incurrence of a liability for the purpose of acquiring:

* Assets,
* Goods, or
* Services.

In simple terms:

> **Expenditure is the amount spent or obligation incurred to obtain something for the business.**

### Examples

* Purchasing machinery for ₹5,00,000
* Purchasing goods for resale for ₹1,00,000
* Paying rent of ₹20,000
* Purchasing office supplies for ₹5,000

All of these involve expenditure.

### Important point

**Expenditure does not automatically mean expense.**

This is one of the most important distinctions in this chapter.

An expenditure may result in:

* an asset being acquired, or
* an expense being recognised.

---

# 2. Capital Expenditure vs Revenue Expenditure

Expenditure is broadly divided into:

$$
\boxed{
\text{Capital Expenditure}
\quad\text{and}\quad
\text{Revenue Expenditure}
}
$$

The distinction is based mainly on the **nature and benefit of the expenditure**.

---

# 2.1 Capital Expenditure

## Meaning

**Capital expenditure** is expenditure incurred to:

* acquire a long-term/permanent asset, or
* improve an existing long-term asset,

where the asset is **not intended for resale in the ordinary course of business**.

It can therefore increase the value or productive capacity of a business asset.

### Examples

* Purchase of machinery
* Purchase of land
* Purchase of building
* Major expenditure that improves the capacity of an existing machine

### Key idea

Capital expenditure is associated with **creating or improving a long-term productive resource**.

---

## Characteristics of Capital Expenditure

### 1. Usually non-recurring

It is generally incurred occasionally rather than continuously.

For example, a company may purchase a new machine once every several years.

### 2. Long-term benefit

The benefit extends beyond the current accounting year.

### 3. Increases earning capacity

Capital expenditure may increase the productive or earning capacity of the business.

### 4. Initially associated with the Balance Sheet

The amount is generally capitalised as part of the relevant asset rather than being charged entirely to the current year's Profit and Loss Account.

### 5. Benefit extends over several years

For a depreciable asset, the cost is generally allocated over its useful life through **depreciation**.

---

## Example

Suppose a company purchases machinery for:

$$
₹10,00,000
$$

The machine is expected to be used for 10 years.

The entire ₹10 lakh is not normally treated as the expense of the year in which the machine is purchased.

Instead, the machinery is recognised as an asset and its depreciable amount is allocated over its useful life according to the applicable accounting rules.

Conceptually:

$$
\text{Capital expenditure}
\rightarrow
\text{Asset}
\rightarrow
\text{Depreciation over useful life}
$$

---

# 2.2 Revenue Expenditure

## Meaning

**Revenue expenditure** is expenditure incurred in the **normal course of business operations** for generating revenue or maintaining the existing earning capacity of the business.

Examples include:

* Salaries
* Rent
* Electricity
* Routine repairs
* Administrative expenses
* Cost of goods sold
* Ordinary maintenance expenses

### Key idea

Revenue expenditure is generally associated with the **day-to-day running of the business**.

---

## Characteristics of Revenue Expenditure

### 1. Generally recurring

Such expenses are often incurred repeatedly.

For example:

* monthly rent,
* monthly salaries,
* electricity bills.

### 2. Benefit generally consumed within the current period

The benefit usually relates to the current accounting period.

### 3. Maintains earning capacity

Ordinary revenue expenditure generally helps maintain the existing operating capacity rather than creating a new long-term asset.

### 4. Charged to Profit and Loss

Revenue expenditure is generally recognised as an expense in determining the profit or loss of the period, subject to the applicable accounting rules.

---

# 3. Capital Expenditure vs Revenue Expenditure

| Basis                          | Capital Expenditure                               | Revenue Expenditure                                                                                        |
| ------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Purpose**                    | Acquisition/improvement of long-term asset        | Day-to-day operation and maintenance                                                                       |
| **Benefit**                    | Extends over multiple periods                     | Generally consumed in current period                                                                       |
| **Frequency**                  | Usually non-recurring                             | Generally recurring                                                                                        |
| **Effect on earning capacity** | May increase productive/earning capacity          | Generally maintains existing capacity                                                                      |
| **Accounting treatment**       | Capitalised as asset, subject to applicable rules | Generally charged to P&L as expense                                                                        |
| **Balance Sheet**              | Associated with asset recognition                 | Generally does not remain as an asset after recognition, except where another accounting treatment applies |
| **Example**                    | Purchase of machinery                             | Routine repair of machinery                                                                                |

---

# 4. An Important Example

Suppose a company owns a machine.

### Case A — Major improvement

₹2,00,000 is spent to significantly increase the machine's production capacity.

This may qualify as:

$$
\boxed{\text{Capital Expenditure}}
$$

because it improves the asset and provides future economic benefits.

### Case B — Routine repair

₹20,000 is spent on ordinary maintenance.

This is generally:

$$
\boxed{\text{Revenue Expenditure}}
$$

because it maintains the existing operating condition.

### Core distinction

Ask:

> **Does the expenditure create/acquire or substantially improve a long-term resource, or is it simply part of the ordinary operation and maintenance of the business?**

That question is much more useful than simply memorising examples.

---

# 5. Expense

## Meaning

An **expense** is a cost whose economic benefit has been **consumed or expired during the accounting period**.

The lecture states:

> An expense is a value that has expired during the accounting period.

In simple language:

> **Expenditure is the spending/incurrence; expense is the portion whose benefit has been consumed in the period.**

An expense is recognised in determining the **profit or loss** of the period.

---

# 6. Expenditure vs Expense

This is an important distinction.

### Expenditure

Focuses on the **incurrence of a cost / use of resources to acquire something**.

### Expense

Focuses on the **consumption or expiration of economic benefit**.

### Example: Machinery

A machine is purchased for:

$$
₹6,00,000
$$

The purchase represents:

$$
\boxed{\text{Capital Expenditure}}
$$

But the machine is used during the year.

Suppose depreciation for the year is:

$$
₹60,000
$$

Then:

$$
\boxed{₹60,000=\text{Expense}}
$$

The entire ₹6 lakh is not necessarily an expense of the current year merely because ₹6 lakh was spent.

---

# 7. Prepaid Expense

A **prepaid expense** is an amount paid in advance for a benefit that will be received in a future period.

### Example

Suppose annual insurance of ₹1,20,000 is paid in advance.

If ₹30,000 relates to the next accounting year, that ₹30,000 is a **prepaid expense** at the reporting date.

The current year's expense is only the amount relating to the current period.

### Concept

$$
\boxed{
\text{Payment made now}
\neq
\text{Entire amount is current expense}
}
$$

The unused portion represents a future economic benefit and is therefore treated separately.

### Simple timeline

```text
Payment made
     │
     ├──────── Current period ────────┤
     │
     └──────── Future period ─────────┐
                                      │
                           Prepaid amount
```

---

# 8. Outstanding Expense

An **outstanding expense** is an expense:

* relating to the current accounting period,
* whose benefit has already been received/consumed,
* but which has **not yet been paid**.

### Example

Suppose salary of ₹50,000 for March is still unpaid at year-end.

The employees have already provided the service, so the salary is an expense of the current period.

But because payment has not yet been made, it is also an obligation.

Thus:

$$
\boxed{\text{Outstanding Expense}=\text{Expense incurred but not yet paid}}
$$

It is generally recognised as:

* an **expense** in the Income Statement, and
* a **liability** in the Balance Sheet.

---

# 9. Prepaid vs Outstanding Expense

| Basis                | Prepaid Expense                       | Outstanding Expense              |
| -------------------- | ------------------------------------- | -------------------------------- |
| Payment              | Already paid                          | Not yet paid                     |
| Benefit              | Relates partly/fully to future period | Already received/consumed        |
| Current expense      | Only applicable current portion       | Current-period amount is expense |
| Balance Sheet effect | Asset / prepayment                    | Liability                        |
| Example              | Insurance paid in advance             | Salary due but unpaid            |

### Easy memory trick

**Prepaid:**

> **Paid first → benefit later**

**Outstanding:**

> **Benefit first → payment later**

---

# 10. Income

The lecture defines **income** as the profit earned during a period.

It gives:

$$
\boxed{\text{Income}=\text{Revenue}-\text{Expense}}
$$

For conceptual clarity, in formal accounting terminology **income is broader than profit**. It can include revenue as well as gains, depending on the accounting framework.

For the introductory treatment in this lecture, the important relationship is:

$$
\boxed{\text{Profit}=\text{Revenue}-\text{Expenses}}
$$

---

# 11. Revenue

Although the screenshot begins to introduce revenue through the examples, it is useful to distinguish it clearly.

**Revenue** is income arising from the entity's **ordinary activities**, such as:

* Sale of goods
* Rendering of services

### Example

A furniture business sells furniture worth:

$$
₹5,00,000
$$

The ₹5 lakh generated from its ordinary sales activity is:

$$
\boxed{\text{Revenue}}
$$

---

# 12. Profit

## Meaning

**Profit** is the excess of revenue over the related costs/expenses.

In simplified form:

$$
\boxed{
\text{Profit}=\text{Revenue}-\text{Expenses}
}
$$

### Example

Revenue:

$$
₹8,00,000
$$

Expenses:

$$
₹6,00,000
$$

Therefore:

$$
\text{Profit}=₹8,00,000-₹6,00,000
$$

$$
\boxed{\text{Profit}=₹2,00,000}
$$

Profit generally increases the owner's equity.

$$
\boxed{\text{Profit} \Rightarrow \text{Increase in Equity}}
$$

---

# 13. Gain

A **gain** is a profit arising from an activity that is generally **incidental, irregular or non-recurring**, rather than from the ordinary sale of goods or services.

### Example from the lecture

A machine has a carrying/cost amount of:

$$
₹25,000
$$

and is sold for:

$$
₹28,000
$$

Therefore:

$$
\text{Gain}=₹28,000-₹25,000
$$

$$
\boxed{\text{Gain}=₹3,000}
$$

The business's ordinary activity may be manufacturing or selling furniture—not selling machinery.

Therefore, the ₹3,000 is a **gain**, rather than ordinary sales revenue.

---

# 14. Loss

A **loss** occurs when expenses/costs exceed the related revenue, or when an adverse event causes a reduction in economic benefits.

The lecture describes loss as an excess of expenses over related revenues.

### Example

Revenue:

$$
₹4,00,000
$$

Expenses:

$$
₹4,80,000
$$

Then:

$$
\text{Loss}=₹4,80,000-₹4,00,000
$$

$$
\boxed{\text{Loss}=₹80,000}
$$

A loss decreases owner's equity.

$$
\boxed{\text{Loss} \Rightarrow \text{Decrease in Equity}}
$$

---

# 15. Revenue vs Gain

This distinction is particularly important.

| Revenue                                     | Gain                                                                |
| ------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Arises from ordinary activities             | Usually arises from incidental/non-core activities                  |
| Recurring in nature in many businesses      | Often irregular/non-recurring                                       |
| Example: sale of goods by a trading company | Profit on sale of machinery                                         |
| Part of ordinary operating income           | Arises from transactions outside normal revenue-generating activity |

### Example

For a furniture business:

**Selling furniture**

$$
\boxed{\text{Revenue}}
$$

**Selling an old machine above its carrying amount**

$$
\boxed{\text{Gain}}
$$

---

# 16. Profit vs Gain

A useful conceptual distinction is:

$$
\boxed{
\text{Profit = broader result of operations}
}
$$

$$
\boxed{
\text{Gain = increase in economic benefit from an incidental/non-core event}
}
$$

A gain may contribute to overall profit.

So they are not necessarily competing concepts.

For example:

$$
\text{Operating Revenue}
-\text{Operating Expenses}
+\text{Gain}
-\text{Loss}
=\text{Overall Profit}
$$

subject to the accounting framework and other income/expense items.

---

# 17. Loss vs Expense

These two terms are also different.

### Expense

An expense generally arises from the **consumption of resources in carrying out normal business activities**.

Examples:

* Salary
* Rent
* Electricity

### Loss

A loss often represents an **unfavourable outcome**, particularly from an incidental event or when expenses exceed related income.

Examples:

* Loss due to theft
* Loss on sale of machinery
* Loss due to damage from an unexpected event

Thus:

> **Expense is generally associated with the cost of earning revenue, whereas loss represents an unfavourable economic outcome.**

---

# 18. Case Study: Identifying Terms

The lecture gives examples for a furniture business.

| Transaction / Event                        | Appropriate Term     | Reason                                                        |
| ------------------------------------------ | -------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Sold goods worth ₹50,000 to Mr. X          | **Sales / Revenue**  | Ordinary sale of business goods                               |
| Theft of cash from office                  | **Loss**             | Unfavourable reduction in resources                           |
| Sold goods costing ₹10,000 for ₹12,000     | **Revenue + Profit** | ₹12,000 sales revenue; ₹2,000 gross profit before other costs |
| Machinery costing ₹25,000 sold for ₹28,000 | **Gain**             | ₹3,000 gain on disposal of non-current asset                  |

### Important calculation

For the goods:

$$
\text{Sales}=₹12,000
$$

$$
\text{Cost}=₹10,000
$$

Therefore:

$$
\boxed{\text{Profit}=₹2,000}
$$

For machinery:

$$
\text{Sale proceeds}=₹28,000
$$

$$
\text{Carrying amount/cost given}=₹25,000
$$

Thus:

$$
\boxed{\text{Gain}=₹3,000}
$$

# 19. Terms Related to Trade

This section introduces the vocabulary used for buying and selling goods.

---

## 19.1 Purchases

**Purchases** means buying goods in which the business **ordinarily deals**, for the purpose of resale.

### Example

A furniture trader buys furniture worth ₹1,00,000 for resale.

$$
\boxed{\text{Purchases}=₹1,00,000}
$$

### Important distinction

If the furniture trader buys a computer for office use, that is generally **not a purchase of goods for the trading account**; it is acquisition of an asset.

So "purchase" in the accounting sense often refers specifically to **goods bought for resale** in a trading business.

---

# 20. Purchase Return / Returns Outward

When goods previously purchased are returned by the business to its supplier, it is called:

* **Purchase Return**, or
* **Returns Outward**

### Example

Goods worth ₹10,000 were purchased from a supplier.

Later, defective goods worth ₹2,000 are returned.

Then:

$$
\boxed{\text{Purchase Returns}=₹2,000}
$$

This reduces the effective purchases.

$$
\boxed{
\text{Net Purchases}
=
\text{Purchases}
-
\text{Purchase Returns}
}
$$

---

# 21. Sales

**Sales** refers to the transfer/sale of goods or services to customers for consideration.

For a trading business, sales generally means the sale of goods in which the business deals.

### Example

A furniture trader sells goods worth:

$$
₹50,000
$$

This is:

$$
\boxed{\text{Sales}=₹50,000}
$$

Sales are a major source of **revenue** for a trading business.

---

# 22. Sales Return / Returns Inward

When customers return goods previously sold by the business, it is called:

* **Sales Return**, or
* **Returns Inward**

### Example

Goods sold for ₹20,000 are later returned by the customer for ₹3,000.

Then:

$$
\boxed{\text{Sales Returns}=₹3,000}
$$

and:

$$
\boxed{
\text{Net Sales}
=
\text{Sales}
-
\text{Sales Returns}
}
$$

### Memory trick

**Returns Outward → Purchase Return**

Goods go **out of the business** back to the supplier.

**Returns Inward → Sales Return**

Goods come **in to the business** from the customer.

---

# 23. Purchases vs Sales

| Term                                  | Meaning                     |
| ------------------------------------- | --------------------------- |
| **Purchases**                         | Goods bought for resale     |
| **Purchase Return / Returns Outward** | Goods returned to supplier  |
| **Sales**                             | Goods sold to customers     |
| **Sales Return / Returns Inward**     | Goods returned by customers |

A useful flow is:

$$
\text{Supplier}
\xrightarrow{\text{Goods}}
\text{Business}
\xrightarrow{\text{Goods}}
\text{Customer}
$$

Returns reverse these flows.

---

# 24. Stock

The lecture defines **stock** as goods purchased for resale that remain unsold at the end of the accounting period.

Thus, in the context of a trading business:

$$
\boxed{
\text{Stock}=\text{Goods held for resale that remain unsold}
}
$$

### Example

A trader purchases goods worth ₹5,00,000.

By year-end:

* Goods sold = ₹4,00,000
* Goods remaining = ₹1,00,000

Then the closing stock is:

$$
\boxed{₹1,00,000}
$$

subject to the applicable inventory measurement rules.

---

# 25. Inventory

**Inventory** is a broader term than stock in the traditional terminology used in the lecture.

It can include:

1. Raw materials
2. Semi-finished / work-in-progress goods
3. Finished goods
4. Stock held for resale

Thus:

$$
\boxed{
\text{Inventory} \supset \text{Stock}
}
$$

in the lecture's terminology.

---

## 25.1 Raw Material Inventory

These are materials that will be used in production.

### Example

For a furniture manufacturer:

* Wood
* Nails
* Glue
* Other production materials

are raw-material inventory.

---

## 25.2 Semi-Finished Goods / Work in Progress

These are goods that have entered the production process but are **not yet completely finished**.

### Example

A partially assembled table at year-end is work-in-progress.

---

## 25.3 Finished Goods

These are goods that have completed production but are still held by the business for sale.

### Example

Completed tables waiting to be sold.

---

## 25.4 Stock

In the traditional trading terminology of the lecture, stock refers particularly to:

> **Finished/trading goods held for resale and remaining unsold.**

---

# 26. Stock vs Inventory

| Stock                                  | Inventory                                                |
| -------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------- |
| Narrower term in the lecture           | Broader term                                             |
| Mainly refers to goods held for resale | Includes raw materials, WIP, finished goods and stock    |
| Particularly relevant to trading goods | Particularly useful for manufacturing businesses as well |

### Example

For a furniture manufacturer:

$$
\text{Raw materials}
+
\text{WIP}
+
\text{Finished goods}
=
\text{Inventory}
$$

The finished goods held for sale may be called **stock** in the traditional terminology.

---

# 27. Bills Receivable

A **Bill Receivable** refers to a **bill of exchange** drawn on a debtor or received by the business by endorsement from another party, under which the specified amount is receivable at a future date.

In simple terms:

> **A bill receivable represents a formal written right to receive money in the future.**

### Example

A customer owes the business ₹50,000.

Instead of simply having an informal credit balance, the customer accepts a bill promising to pay ₹50,000 after 3 months.

For the business:

$$
\boxed{\text{Bill Receivable}=₹50,000}
$$

It represents an amount that the business expects to **receive in the future**.

---

# 28. Bills Payable

A **Bill Payable** is a bill of exchange **accepted by the business in favour of a creditor**, under which the business must pay the specified amount at a future date.

In simple terms:

> **A bill payable represents a formal obligation of the business to pay money in the future.**

### Example

The business owes a supplier:

$$
₹60,000
$$

The business accepts a bill payable after three months.

For the business:

$$
\boxed{\text{Bill Payable}=₹60,000}
$$

This is a liability.

---

# 29. Bills Receivable vs Bills Payable

| Bills Receivable                  | Bills Payable                         |
| --------------------------------- | ------------------------------------- |
| Amount to be **received**         | Amount to be **paid**                 |
| Asset in the usual classification | Liability in the usual classification |
| Usually arises from debtors       | Usually arises from creditors         |
| Gives right to receive money      | Creates obligation to pay money       |
| Future inflow                     | Future outflow                        |

### Easy memory trick

$$
\boxed{\text{Receivable = Receive}}
$$

$$
\boxed{\text{Payable = Pay}}
$$

---

# 30. Debtors

**Debtors** are persons or firms to whom the business has sold goods or provided services **on credit**, and from whom payment has not yet been received.

Thus:

$$
\boxed{\text{Debtors owe money to the business}}
$$

### Example

A business sells goods worth ₹1,00,000 to X on credit.

Until X pays:

$$
\boxed{\text{X is a debtor}}
$$

The amount is generally shown as a **trade receivable**.

---

# 31. Creditors

**Creditors** are persons or firms from whom the business has:

* purchased goods, or
* obtained services

on credit, and to whom payment is still due.

Thus:

$$
\boxed{\text{Creditors are owed money by the business}}
$$

### Example

A business purchases goods worth ₹80,000 from Y on credit.

Until the business pays Y:

$$
\boxed{\text{Y is a creditor}}
$$

The amount is generally a **trade payable**.

---

# 32. Debtor vs Creditor

This is one of the easiest places to get confused.

### Debtor

$$
\boxed{\text{Someone who owes us money}}
$$

### Creditor

$$
\boxed{\text{Someone whom we owe money}}
$$

### Example

Suppose:

**A sells ₹20,000 of goods on credit to B.**

For A:

$$
\boxed{B=\text{Debtor}}
$$

Now suppose:

**A buys ₹15,000 of goods on credit from C.**

For A:

$$
\boxed{C=\text{Creditor}}
$$

### Memory trick

> **Debtor → Due to us**

> **Creditor → Credit given to us**

---

# 33. Bad Debts

A **bad debt** is an amount due from a debtor that has become **irrecoverable**.

### Example

A customer owes:

$$
₹50,000
$$

but becomes unable to pay, and the amount is determined to be irrecoverable.

Then:

$$
\boxed{\text{Bad Debt}=₹50,000}
$$

It represents a loss to the business.

The lecture states that bad debts are charged to the **Profit and Loss Account as an expense/loss**.

### Effect

$$
\text{Debtor/Receivable} \downarrow
$$

$$
\text{Profit} \downarrow
$$

$$
\text{Equity} \downarrow
$$

---

# 34. Bad Debt Example

Suppose:

$$
\text{Debtors}=₹3,00,000
$$

A debtor owing:

$$
₹20,000
$$

becomes insolvent and the amount cannot be recovered.

Then:

$$
\text{Bad debt}=₹20,000
$$

and remaining receivables become:

$$
₹3,00,000-₹20,000
=
₹2,80,000
$$

The ₹20,000 is recognised as a loss/expense according to the applicable accounting treatment.

---

# 35. Insolvent / Insolvency

A person or business is described as **insolvent** when they are unable to meet their debts/obligations as they fall due, or, depending on the legal framework, when their liabilities exceed their assets.

The lecture gives the simple definition:

> A person or enterprise which is not in a position to pay its debts.

### Relationship to bad debt

Suppose:

* Business A is owed money by Customer B.
* Customer B becomes insolvent.
* B cannot repay the amount.

Then A may suffer a:

$$
\boxed{\text{Bad Debt}}
$$

So:

$$
\text{Insolvency of debtor}
\rightarrow
\text{Possible irrecoverability}
\rightarrow
\text{Bad debt for creditor}
$$

---

# 36. Discount

A **discount** is a rebate or allowance given by the seller to the buyer.

The lecture divides discount into two types:

$$
\boxed{
\text{Trade Discount}
\quad+\quad
\text{Cash Discount}
}
$$

These should be clearly distinguished.

---

# 37. Trade Discount

## Meaning

A **trade discount** is a discount given at a stated percentage on the **list/catalogue price** of goods.

It is generally given for commercial reasons such as:

* bulk purchases,
* dealer arrangements,
* regular trade relationships,
* wholesale pricing.

### Accounting treatment

The lecture highlights:

> **Trade discount is not recorded separately in the books of account.**

Instead, it is deducted from the list price when determining the invoice amount.

### Example

List price:

$$
₹1,00,000
$$

Trade discount:

$$
10\%
$$

Discount:

$$
₹1,00,000\times10\%
=
₹10,000
$$

Invoice value:

$$
₹1,00,000-₹10,000
=
₹90,000
$$

The transaction is generally recorded at:

$$
\boxed{₹90,000}
$$

rather than recording ₹1,00,000 purchase and ₹10,000 trade discount separately.

---

# 38. Cash Discount

A **cash discount** is an allowance given for **prompt or early payment**.

Unlike trade discount, the lecture emphasises that:

$$
\boxed{\text{Cash discount is recorded in the books}}
$$

### Example

Amount due:

$$
₹50,000
$$

Cash discount allowed for prompt payment:

$$
5\%
$$

Discount:

$$
₹50,000\times5\%
=
₹2,500
$$

Cash actually paid:

$$
₹50,000-₹2,500
=
₹47,500
$$

For the seller:

$$
\boxed{\text{Discount Allowed}}
$$

For the buyer:

$$
\boxed{\text{Discount Received}}
$$

---

# 39. Trade Discount vs Cash Discount

| Basis                    | Trade Discount                        | Cash Discount                                   |
| ------------------------ | ------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------- |
| **Purpose**              | Commercial/quantity/dealer discount   | Prompt payment                                  |
| **When given**           | Usually at the time of sale/invoicing | At the time of settlement/payment               |
| **Recorded separately?** | Generally **No**                      | **Yes**                                         |
| **Shown in invoice?**    | Usually deducted from list price      | Usually depends on payment terms and settlement |
| **Example**              | 10% wholesale discount                | 5% discount for paying within 10 days           |

### Easy memory trick

**Trade discount**

$$
\boxed{\text{Discount on price}}
$$

**Cash discount**

$$
\boxed{\text{Discount on payment}}
$$

---

# 40. Comprehensive Example Combining the Terms

Suppose a furniture business receives a catalogue price of:

$$
₹1,00,000
$$

from a supplier.

The supplier gives a trade discount of 10%.

Therefore:

$$
\text{Trade Discount}=₹10,000
$$

and invoice value:

$$
₹90,000
$$

Suppose the furniture is purchased on credit.

Then:

$$
\boxed{\text{Purchases}=₹90,000}
$$

and:

$$
\boxed{\text{Creditor}=₹90,000}
$$

Later, the business returns furniture worth ₹10,000.

Then:

$$
\boxed{\text{Purchase Return}=₹10,000}
$$

Remaining payable:

$$
₹90,000-₹10,000
=
₹80,000
$$

Suppose the supplier allows a 5% cash discount for immediate settlement.

Then:

$$
\text{Cash Discount}
=
₹80,000\times5\%
=
₹4,000
$$

Cash paid:

$$
₹80,000-₹4,000
=
₹76,000
$$

This one example connects:

$$
\boxed{
\text{Trade Discount}
\rightarrow
\text{Purchases}
\rightarrow
\text{Purchase Return}
\rightarrow
\text{Creditor}
\rightarrow
\text{Cash Discount}
}
$$

# 45. Exam-Focused Final Revision

The terms most worth learning **exactly and distinctly** from Part 2_B are:

### Expenditure

Amount spent or liability incurred to acquire assets, goods or services.

### Capital Expenditure

Expenditure incurred to acquire or substantially improve long-term assets and obtain benefits over multiple periods.

### Revenue Expenditure

Expenditure incurred in the normal course of business and generally consumed in the current period.

### Expense

A cost whose benefit has been consumed or expired during the accounting period.

### Prepaid Expense

Paid now, benefit received later.

### Outstanding Expense

Benefit received now, payment made later.

### Revenue

Income generated from ordinary business activities.

### Profit

Excess of revenue over expenses.

### Gain

Profit arising from an incidental/non-core transaction, such as sale of a fixed asset above its carrying amount.

### Loss

An unfavourable economic outcome or excess of expenses over related revenue.

### Purchases

Goods bought for resale.

### Purchase Return / Returns Outward

Goods returned to suppliers.

### Sales

Goods sold to customers.

### Sales Return / Returns Inward

Goods returned by customers.

### Stock

Unsold goods held for resale.

### Inventory

Broader category including raw materials, work-in-progress, finished goods and stock.

### Debtor

Person/business that owes money **to us**.

### Creditor

Person/business **to whom we owe money**.

### Bills Receivable

Money to be **received** in the future under a bill.

### Bills Payable

Money to be **paid** in the future under a bill.

### Bad Debt

Amount due from a debtor that becomes irrecoverable.

### Insolvent

Unable to meet debts/obligations.

### Trade Discount

Reduction from list price; generally not separately recorded.

### Cash Discount

Reduction for prompt payment; recorded in the books.

# Basics of Accounting — Part 2_C

## Accounting Principles, Concepts and Conventions

Part 2_C is one of the most important theoretical sections in the introductory accounting module. It explains the **principles and assumptions that govern the preparation of accounting records and financial statements**.

The broad sequence is:

$$
\boxed{
\text{Accounting Principles}
\rightarrow
\text{Accounting Concepts}
\rightarrow
\text{Accounting Conventions}
}
$$

These concepts explain **how transactions should be recognised, measured, recorded and presented** so that accounting information is meaningful and comparable.

---

# 1. Accounting Principles

## 1.1 Meaning

Financial statements are produced from a very large volume of information about the economic activities of an enterprise.

This information is:

$$
\text{Accumulated}
\rightarrow
\text{Analysed}
\rightarrow
\text{Recorded}
\rightarrow
\text{Reported}
$$

This process should be carried out according to established **accounting principles**.

### Definition

> **Accounting principles are the basic rules, concepts and guidelines that provide the foundation for recording transactions and preparing financial statements.**

They provide guidance regarding:

* how transactions should be recorded,
* how financial information should be measured,
* what information should be disclosed,
* how financial statements should be prepared,
* and how accounting information should be presented to users.

---

# 2. Generally Accepted Accounting Principles (GAAP)

## Meaning

**GAAP** stands for:

$$
\boxed{\text{Generally Accepted Accounting Principles}}
$$

GAAP refers broadly to the accepted body of accounting principles, standards and practices used within a particular jurisdiction.

It provides a framework for detailed accounting rules and reporting practices.

The lecture describes GAAP as encompassing:

1. **Basic accounting principles/guidelines**
2. **Accounting standards** issued by the relevant accounting standard-setting body
3. **Industry-specific accounting practices**, where required for unusual or specialised situations

### Important clarification

GAAP is **not one identical worldwide rulebook**. Accounting frameworks differ by jurisdiction. For example, countries may use different standards or adaptations of international standards.

So for conceptual purposes:

$$
\boxed{
\text{GAAP}=\text{Generally accepted framework of accounting rules and practices within a jurisdiction}
}
$$

---

# 3. Why Accounting Principles are Needed

Without common principles, two businesses could record the same economic event in completely different ways.

That would make financial statements difficult to:

* compare,
* understand,
* analyse,
* audit,
* and use for decision-making.

Accounting principles therefore promote:

### Consistency

Similar transactions are treated in similar ways.

### Comparability

Financial statements of different periods or entities can be meaningfully compared.

### Reliability and credibility

Users have a structured basis for understanding the information.

### Proper presentation

Financial statements follow an organised framework rather than arbitrary treatment.

---

# 4. Separate Business Entity Concept

## Meaning

The **Separate Business Entity Concept** states that the **business and its owner are treated as separate entities for accounting purposes**.

Even when a business is owned by an individual, the accounting records are maintained from the **point of view of the business**.

Therefore:

$$
\boxed{\text{Business} \neq \text{Owner}}
$$

for accounting purposes.

---

## 4.1 Example: Owner Invests Money

Suppose the owner introduces:

$$
₹1,00,000
$$

into the business.

From the owner's personal perspective, the owner has given money to the business.

From the **business's perspective**, the business has received ₹1 lakh and the owner's claim is recorded as:

$$
\boxed{\text{Capital} = ₹1,00,000}
$$

Thus:

$$
\text{Business receives cash}
$$

and:

$$
\text{Owner's Equity/Capital increases}
$$

---

## 4.2 Example: Owner Withdraws Money

Suppose the owner withdraws:

$$
₹20,000
$$

for personal use.

The business records this as:

$$
\boxed{\text{Drawings}=₹20,000}
$$

It is not treated as a business expense merely because the business's cash has decreased.

This is a direct consequence of the separate-entity concept.

---

## 4.3 Why this concept matters

Without this separation, it would be difficult to determine:

* how much belongs to the business,
* how much belongs to the owner personally,
* what the actual business expenses are,
* what the business's actual financial position is.

Thus:

> **Personal transactions of the owner should not be mixed with business transactions unless accounting principles specifically require recognition.**

---

# 5. Money Measurement Concept

## Meaning

The **Money Measurement Concept** states that accounting records only those transactions and events that can be **expressed in monetary terms**.

Thus:

$$
\boxed{
\text{Recordable accounting information}
\Rightarrow
\text{Measurable in money}
}
$$

A fact may be important to the business but still not be directly recorded in the accounting books if it cannot be reliably expressed in monetary terms.

---

## 5.1 Example

Suppose a company has an exceptionally skilled management team.

This may be very valuable to the business, but the quality of the management team itself is not ordinarily recorded as a monetary asset simply because it is difficult to assign a sufficiently objective accounting value.

Similarly, the lecture gives the example of the **general health condition of a Managing Director**.

It could affect the business, but it is not normally recorded as an accounting transaction because it is not a monetary transaction.

---

# 6. Limitations of the Money Measurement Concept

The concept creates two important limitations.

## 6.1 Qualitative information may be excluded

Important facts that cannot be expressed reliably in money may not appear directly in the accounting records.

Examples:

* Employee morale
* Quality of management
* Reputation
* Industrial relations
* Health of key personnel
* Customer loyalty

Hence:

$$
\boxed{
\text{Financial statements do not capture every economically relevant fact}
}
$$

---

## 6.2 Assumption of a stable monetary unit

When transactions are measured in money, accounting generally assumes that the monetary unit provides a sufficiently stable basis of measurement.

The lecture expresses this as assuming a:

> **stable or constant value of the rupee**

In reality, the purchasing power of money can change because of inflation and other economic conditions.

For example:

₹1 lakh today does not necessarily have the same purchasing power as ₹1 lakh several years ago.

This is one reason historical financial information may sometimes need careful interpretation.

---

# 7. Dual Aspect Concept

## Meaning

The **Dual Aspect Concept** states that every business transaction has **two accounting aspects/effects**.

This is the conceptual foundation of the **double-entry bookkeeping system**.

In simplified form:

$$
\boxed{
\text{Every transaction has at least two effects}
}
$$

These two effects keep the accounting equation balanced.

---

# 8. Example of Dual Aspect

Suppose a business makes a cash sale of:

$$
₹10,000
$$

Two things happen:

### Aspect 1: Goods are transferred to the customer

The business's inventory decreases.

### Aspect 2: Cash is received

The business's cash increases.

Thus, one transaction produces two effects.

---

## 8.1 Accounting equation perspective

For a simplified transaction:

$$
\text{Cash} \uparrow
$$

and:

$$
\text{Sales/Income} \uparrow
$$

Since income ultimately affects equity:

$$
\text{Equity} \uparrow
$$

The accounting equation remains balanced.

---

# 9. Another Example of Dual Aspect

Suppose the business purchases furniture for:

$$
₹50,000
$$

for cash.

Two effects:

$$
\text{Furniture} \uparrow ₹50,000
$$

$$
\text{Cash} \downarrow ₹50,000
$$

Total assets do not necessarily change:

$$
+₹50,000-₹50,000=0
$$

But their **composition changes**.

This is an excellent example of why "dual aspect" does not mean that the total assets must always increase or decrease.

It means that the transaction has **two corresponding effects**.

---

# 10. Accounting Equation and Dual Aspect

The dual aspect concept leads directly to:

$$
\boxed{
\text{Assets}=\text{Liabilities}+\text{Equity}
}
$$

or, in the traditional terminology used by the course:

$$
\boxed{
\text{Assets}=\text{Liabilities}+\text{Capital}
}
$$

Every transaction must affect the accounting equation in such a way that it remains balanced.

This is why the concept is fundamental to double-entry accounting.

---

# 11. Going Concern Concept

## Meaning

The **Going Concern Concept** assumes that the business will continue its operations for the foreseeable future rather than being forced to liquidate in the immediate future.

In simple terms:

$$
\boxed{
\text{Business is assumed to continue operating}
}
$$

unless there is evidence to the contrary.

---

## 11.1 Why is this assumption necessary?

Suppose a machine is purchased for:

$$
₹10,00,000
$$

and is expected to be used for 10 years.

If the business is assumed to continue operating, it makes sense to treat the machine as providing benefits over its useful life and allocate its cost through depreciation.

But if the business were about to shut down immediately, the relevant question might instead be:

> What amount can actually be recovered from selling the machine?

Therefore, the going-concern assumption influences the way assets and liabilities are measured and presented.

---

# 12. Implications of Going Concern

Because the business is assumed to continue:

* Assets are generally not treated as though they must all be sold immediately.
* Long-term assets can be used over their useful lives.
* Costs can be allocated across accounting periods.
* Depreciation can be charged over the useful life of assets.
* Long-term liabilities can be classified and presented according to their contractual terms.

### Core idea

$$
\boxed{
\text{Going concern}
\Rightarrow
\text{Business is valued/accounted for as an operating entity}
}
$$

rather than an entity that is about to be liquidated.

---

# 13. Accounting Period Concept

## Meaning

A business may theoretically continue indefinitely.

But users cannot wait indefinitely to know its financial performance.

Therefore, the life of a business is divided into **accounting periods**.

Common periods include:

* Month
* Quarter
* Six months
* Financial year

The **year** is a common reporting period.

---

## 13.1 Why divide business life into periods?

Suppose a business starts in 2020 and continues until 2050.

If accounts were prepared only in 2050, the owner would have to wait 30 years to know the overall result.

Instead, the business prepares accounts periodically:

$$
2020 \rightarrow 2021 \rightarrow 2022 \rightarrow \cdots
$$

This allows users to determine:

* annual profit/loss,
* annual expenses,
* financial position at year-end,
* changes in performance over time.

---

# 14. Financial Year vs Calendar Year

The lecture notes that businesses may use different reporting periods depending on:

* prevailing practice,
* tradition,
* government requirements,
* applicable regulations.

### Calendar year

$$
1\text{ January} \rightarrow 31\text{ December}
$$

### Financial year

A financial year may use a different 12-month period, depending on the jurisdiction and reporting requirements.

The important principle is:

> **The business's continuous life is divided into defined accounting periods for reporting purposes.**

---

# 15. Historical Cost Concept

## Meaning

Under the **Historical Cost Concept**, an asset is ordinarily initially recorded at the **cost incurred to acquire it**, rather than simply at its market value on the purchase date.

### Example from the lecture

Suppose a business purchases a plant for:

$$
₹5,00,000
$$

At the time of purchase its market value happens to be:

$$
₹6,00,000
$$

Under the simplified historical-cost principle:

$$
\boxed{\text{Recorded cost}=₹5,00,000}
$$

because ₹5 lakh is the acquisition cost.

---

# 16. Historical Cost Does Not Mean "Never Changes"

This is an important point.

Historical cost does **not** mean that an asset will always appear at exactly its original cost forever.

For example, depreciable assets may be reduced through:

$$
\boxed{\text{Depreciation}}
$$

Suppose:

$$
\text{Original cost}=₹5,00,000
$$

and cumulative depreciation becomes:

$$
₹1,50,000
$$

Then the carrying amount may be:

$$
₹5,00,000-₹1,50,000
=
₹3,50,000
$$

subject to the relevant accounting rules.

So:

$$
\boxed{
\text{Historical Cost} \neq \text{Permanent unchanging book value}
}
$$

---

# 17. Why Historical Cost is Useful

Historical cost has some advantages:

* It is based on an actual transaction.
* It is generally easier to verify.
* It provides objective evidence of the original acquisition amount.
* It reduces dependence on subjective market estimates at the date of acquisition.

However, it can become less representative of current economic value as prices change.

---

# 18. Matching Concept

## Meaning

The **Matching Concept** requires that the revenue earned during an accounting period be matched with the **corresponding expenses/expenditure relating to that revenue** so that the proper profit for the period can be determined.

In simplified form:

$$
\boxed{
\text{Profit}
=
\text{Revenue of period}
-
\text{Expenses relating to that revenue}
}
$$

---

# 19. Example of Matching Concept

Suppose a shop purchases inventory for:

$$
₹1,00,000
$$

during the year.

But by year-end, only goods costing:

$$
₹60,000
$$

have been sold.

The full ₹1,00,000 should not automatically be treated as the cost of the current period's sales.

Instead:

$$
₹60,000
$$

is associated with goods sold, while the remaining:

$$
₹40,000
$$

relates to inventory still held.

Thus the cost associated with the unsold goods remains part of inventory, subject to the applicable inventory accounting rules.

### Central idea

> **Match the cost with the revenue it helped generate.**

---

# 20. Matching and Profit Measurement

Suppose:

$$
\text{Revenue}=₹2,00,000
$$

and related expenses are:

$$
₹1,20,000
$$

Then:

$$
\text{Profit}=₹2,00,000-₹1,20,000
$$

$$
\boxed{\text{Profit}=₹80,000}
$$

The purpose of matching is therefore to ensure that the profit reported for a particular period is not distorted by assigning unrelated expenses to that period.

---

# 21. Accrual Concept

## Meaning

The **Accrual Concept** states that transactions are recognised based on when **income is earned and expenses are incurred**, rather than simply when cash is received or paid.

Thus:

$$
\boxed{
\text{Revenue} \rightarrow \text{when earned}
}
$$

$$
\boxed{
\text{Expense} \rightarrow \text{when incurred}
}
$$

rather than:

$$
\text{Revenue} \rightarrow \text{when cash is received}
$$

or:

$$
\text{Expense} \rightarrow \text{when cash is paid}
$$

---

# 22. Example of Accrual Concept

Suppose the business uses electricity during March.

The electricity bill is received in March but paid in April.

Under accrual accounting, the electricity expense belongs to **March**, because the service was consumed in March.

Therefore:

$$
\boxed{
\text{Expense recognition is based on consumption/incurrence}
}
$$

not the date of payment.

---

# 23. Accrual vs Cash Basis

| Accrual Basis                                | Cash Basis                                         |
| -------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------- |
| Recognise revenue when earned                | Recognise revenue when cash is received            |
| Recognise expense when incurred              | Recognise expense when cash is paid                |
| Focuses on economic activity                 | Focuses on cash movement                           |
| Gives a fuller picture of period performance | Can differ substantially from economic performance |

### Example

A customer buys goods on credit in March and pays in April.

Under accrual accounting:

$$
\text{Revenue is recognised in March}
$$

even though:

$$
\text{Cash is received in April}
$$

---

# 24. Relationship Between Matching and Accrual

These concepts are closely connected.

### Accrual asks:

> **When should the transaction's income or expense be recognised?**

### Matching asks:

> **Which expenses belong with the revenue of this period?**

Together they help determine an economically meaningful measure of periodic profit.

---

# 25. Accounting Concepts vs Accounting Conventions

The lecture distinguishes the two.

| Accounting Concepts                                                           | Accounting Conventions                                           |
| ----------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Fundamental accounting assumptions/principles used in recording and reporting | Generally accepted customs or practices used as practical guides |
| Form the conceptual foundation                                                | Help deal with practical reporting situations                    |
| More fundamental in nature                                                    | Often arise from accepted practice                               |
| Guide recognition/measurement/reporting                                       | Guide consistent presentation and treatment                      |

### Simple distinction

$$
\boxed{\text{Concept}=\text{Fundamental accounting idea/principle}}
$$

$$
\boxed{\text{Convention}=\text{Widely accepted practical practice}}
$$

---

# 26. Materiality Convention

## Meaning

The **Materiality Convention** states that items of **small or insignificant importance** need not always be given the same detailed treatment that would be appropriate for a highly significant item.

The underlying idea is:

> Accounting procedures should be proportionate to the importance of the item.

---

## 26.1 Calculator Example

Suppose an ordinary calculator costs:

$$
₹100
$$

and can physically last for:

$$
10\text{ years}
$$

In theory, its cost could be allocated over the entire useful life.

But the administrative effort involved in:

* classifying it as a long-term asset,
* calculating depreciation for 10 years,
* maintaining detailed records,

may be greater than the benefit obtained from such precision.

Therefore, depending on the entity's accounting policy and materiality threshold, the ₹100 calculator may simply be treated as an expense of the period.

### Core idea

$$
\boxed{
\text{Small/significant?}
\Rightarrow
\text{Use reasonable practical treatment}
}
$$

---

# 27. Materiality is Relative

An item can be material for one business but immaterial for another.

For example:

$$
₹10,000
$$

may be insignificant for a large multinational company but highly significant for a very small business.

Therefore:

$$
\boxed{
\text{Materiality depends on context, size and significance}
}
$$

It is a matter of **judgement**, not merely a universal fixed rupee amount.

---

# 28. Conservatism Convention

The **Conservatism Convention** is based on a cautious approach to accounting.

The lecture expresses the principle as:

> Anticipated expenses or losses should be recognised, while potential income or gains should not be recognised prematurely.

The central idea is:

$$
\boxed{
\text{Do not overstate assets/profits}
}
$$

and:

$$
\boxed{
\text{Recognise probable losses appropriately}
}
$$

---

# 29. Example: Doubtful Debts

Suppose a business has receivables from customers.

There is a reasonable expectation that some portion may not be collected.

Accounting may therefore recognise an appropriate **allowance/provision for doubtful receivables**, rather than assuming that the entire amount will definitely be recovered.

This reflects caution in measuring receivables.

---

# 30. Example: Inventory Valuation

The lecture gives the traditional example:

> Value stock at **cost or market value, whichever is lower**.

The logic is conservative:

Suppose:

$$
\text{Cost}=₹1,00,000
$$

and relevant market value is:

$$
₹90,000
$$

Then under the traditional lower-of-cost-or-market approach:

$$
\boxed{\text{Value}=₹90,000}
$$

This avoids recognising an unrealised increase but recognises a decline in value.

### Important modern clarification

Contemporary inventory accounting typically uses the **lower of cost and net realisable value (NRV)** rather than the old broad "cost or market" formulation. The course material is using the traditional conservatism example.

---

# 31. Conservatism and Gains

Suppose a business owns an asset whose value has increased, but the increase has not been realised or otherwise qualifies for recognition.

A conservative approach does not automatically treat an uncertain/unrealised increase as realised profit simply because the asset could potentially be sold for more.

Thus:

$$
\boxed{
\text{Potential gain} \neq \text{Automatically recognised profit}
}
$$

Recognition depends on the applicable accounting framework.

---

# 32. Consistency Convention

## Meaning

The **Consistency Convention** requires an entity to follow the same accounting policies and methods for similar transactions over successive periods, unless there is a sound reason or applicable requirement to change them.

The objective is to make financial information **comparable over time**.

---

## 32.1 Example: Depreciation

Suppose a company chooses the **straight-line method** of depreciation for an appropriate class of assets.

The method should generally be applied consistently from year to year unless there is a justified reason for changing it, such as a change required or permitted under the applicable accounting framework.

Why?

Because if the company changes methods arbitrarily every year, comparison of profits becomes difficult.

---

# 33. Why Consistency Matters

Suppose:

### Year 1

Depreciation method A is used.

### Year 2

Method B is suddenly used.

### Year 3

Method C is used.

Even if all are individually acceptable under some circumstances, changing methods without justification could make year-to-year performance difficult to compare.

Therefore:

$$
\boxed{
\text{Consistency}
\rightarrow
\text{Comparability}
}
$$

---

# 34. Disclosure Convention

## Meaning

The **Disclosure Convention** requires financial statements to disclose **material information** necessary for users to understand the financial position and performance of the business and make informed decisions.

In simple terms:

> **Material information that could influence users' decisions should not be concealed.**

---

## 34.1 Why disclosure is important

Financial statements contain summarised information.

Users therefore need sufficient additional information to understand:

* accounting policies,
* significant items,
* important uncertainties,
* major commitments,
* relevant risks,
* other material information.

Thus:

$$
\boxed{
\text{Financial statements}
+
\text{Required disclosures}
\rightarrow
\text{Useful information}
}
$$

---

# 39. Important Conceptual Relationships

These concepts are not isolated. They work together.

## Separate Entity + Dual Aspect

The separate entity concept determines **whose books** we are maintaining.

The dual aspect concept determines **how transactions affect those books**.

---

## Going Concern + Historical Cost

Because the business is assumed to continue operating, assets need not necessarily be valued as though they are going to be sold immediately.

Historical cost can therefore be used as a basis for recording assets, followed by applicable depreciation or other adjustments.

---

## Accounting Period + Accrual

The accounting period tells us **which period's performance we are measuring**.

Accrual accounting tells us **which revenues and expenses belong to that period**.

---

## Accrual + Matching

Accrual accounting ensures that income and expenses are recognised in the appropriate period.

Matching then helps ensure that the expenses associated with the relevant revenue are charged to the same period.

Together:

$$
\boxed{
\text{Accrual + Matching}
\rightarrow
\text{Meaningful periodic profit}
}
$$

---

## Consistency + Comparability

$$
\boxed{
\text{Consistent accounting methods}
\rightarrow
\text{Better comparison between periods}
}
$$

---

## Conservatism + Disclosure

Conservatism encourages cautious recognition and measurement.

Disclosure ensures that users receive the material information necessary to understand the resulting figures.

---

# 40. A Complete Worked Example

Consider a business with the following events:

### 1. Owner invests ₹10 lakh

**Separate entity:**

The owner's contribution is recorded as capital/equity of the business.

$$
\text{Cash} \uparrow ₹10,00,000
$$

$$
\text{Capital} \uparrow ₹10,00,000
$$

---

### 2. Business buys machinery for ₹4 lakh

**Dual aspect:**

$$
\text{Machinery} \uparrow ₹4,00,000
$$

$$
\text{Cash} \downarrow ₹4,00,000
$$

**Historical cost:**

Machinery is initially recorded at its acquisition cost:

$$
₹4,00,000
$$

---

### 3. Business purchases inventory for ₹2 lakh

The inventory is initially recognised rather than immediately treating the entire amount as current-period expense.

**Matching concept** becomes relevant when the inventory is later sold.

---

### 4. Goods costing ₹80,000 are sold for ₹1,20,000

Revenue:

$$
₹1,20,000
$$

Related cost:

$$
₹80,000
$$

Therefore:

$$
\text{Gross Profit}=₹1,20,000-₹80,000
$$

$$
\boxed{₹40,000}
$$

This demonstrates the **matching concept**.

---

### 5. Electricity of ₹10,000 relates to December but is paid in January

Under the **accrual concept**:

$$
\boxed{\text{Expense belongs to December}}
$$

even though payment occurs in January.

---

### 6. Some customer balances are doubtful

Under the **conservatism concept**, an appropriate allowance for doubtful receivables may be recognised rather than assuming that every receivable will certainly be collected.

---

### 7. Business uses straight-line depreciation

Under **consistency**, the method should be applied consistently for similar assets unless there is a justified accounting reason to change it.

---

### 8. Financial statements are prepared

Under the **accounting period concept**, the transactions are assigned to the appropriate reporting period.

Under the **disclosure convention**, material information must be properly disclosed.

This example demonstrates how the various concepts operate together rather than independently.

# 42. High-Value Exam Definitions

### Separate Business Entity Concept

> The business is treated as a separate entity from its owner for accounting purposes, and transactions are recorded from the viewpoint of the business.

### Money Measurement Concept

> Only transactions and events capable of being expressed in monetary terms are recognised in the accounting records.

### Dual Aspect Concept

> Every business transaction has two accounting effects, forming the basis of the double-entry system.

### Going Concern Concept

> The business is assumed to continue operating for the foreseeable future and is not expected to liquidate in the immediate future.

### Accounting Period Concept

> The continuous life of a business is divided into appropriate accounting periods for measuring and reporting financial performance.

### Historical Cost Concept

> Assets are initially recognised on the basis of the cost incurred to acquire them, subject to subsequent accounting adjustments.

### Matching Concept

> Revenue of an accounting period should be matched with the corresponding expenses incurred in earning that revenue to determine the proper profit for the period.

### Accrual Concept

> Revenue is recognised when earned and expenses when incurred, rather than merely when cash is received or paid.

### Materiality Convention

> Items that are insignificant in relation to financial statements need not always be subjected to disproportionately detailed accounting treatment.

### Conservatism Convention

> Accounting should avoid premature recognition of uncertain gains while appropriately recognising probable losses and obligations.

### Consistency Convention

> Accounting methods and policies should be applied consistently to similar transactions across periods unless a justified change is required or appropriate.

### Disclosure Convention

> Material information necessary for users to understand the financial statements and make informed decisions should be adequately disclosed.

# 43. Frequently Confused Concepts

## Historical Cost vs Matching

**Historical Cost** asks:

> At what amount should an acquired asset initially be recorded?

**Matching** asks:

> Which costs should be recognised against revenue in this period?

---

## Accrual vs Matching

**Accrual:**

$$
\text{When to recognise?}
$$

**Matching:**

$$
\text{Which expense belongs with which revenue/period?}
$$

---

## Conservatism vs Historical Cost

**Historical Cost** determines the initial basis of measurement.

**Conservatism** deals with caution when uncertainty exists.

---

## Consistency vs Disclosure

**Consistency:**

> Use comparable accounting methods over time.

**Disclosure:**

> Provide users with material information they need to understand the accounts.

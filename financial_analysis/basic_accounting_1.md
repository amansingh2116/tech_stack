# Basics of Accounting — Part 1_A

## Introduction to Accounting

This lecture introduces the **meaning, purpose, functions, and accounting cycle** of accounting. These ideas form the foundation for understanding the later topics in financial analysis.

---

# 1. Introduction to Accounting

## 1.1 Why does a business need accounting?

The fundamental objective of a business is generally to **earn profit** and create value. To determine whether a business is performing well, however, the owner or other stakeholders need reliable financial information.

At the end of an accounting period—such as a month, quarter, or year—a business may want answers to questions such as:

* How much **profit or loss** did the business make?
* How much **inventory/stock** is currently held?
* What are the business's **assets**?
* How much does the business **owe to outsiders**?
* How much money is **owed to the business** by customers or others?
* How much has been **invested by the owners**?
* What are the business's revenues and expenses?
* What is the overall **financial position** of the business?

These questions cannot be answered accurately merely by looking at individual transactions or remembering what happened during the year.

Therefore, a business must maintain a:

> **Complete and systematic record of every business transaction of a financial nature.**

Accounting provides the system through which these transactions are recorded, organized, summarized, analyzed, and communicated.

---

## 1.2 What is a business transaction?

A **business transaction** is an economic event involving the business that can be **measured in monetary terms** and has a financial effect on the business.

Examples include:

* Purchase of goods
* Sale of goods
* Payment of salaries
* Payment of rent
* Purchase of machinery
* Receipt of cash from customers
* Borrowing money from a bank
* Repayment of a loan
* Payment to suppliers

### Important point

Not every event involving a business is necessarily recorded as an accounting transaction.

For example:

> The manager decides to hire an employee next month.

This decision by itself does not necessarily create a measurable financial transaction.

But when the business actually pays the employee's salary, there is a financial transaction that must be recorded.

Thus, accounting primarily records **financially measurable economic events**.

---

# 2. Meaning of Accounting

A standard definition given in the lecture is:

> **Accounting is the process of identifying, recording, classifying, summarising, interpreting and communicating financial information of a business to its users for judgement and decision-making.**

This definition is important because it shows that accounting is **much more than simply recording transactions**.

Accounting involves a sequence of activities:

**Identify → Record → Classify → Summarise → Analyse → Interpret → Communicate**

Each stage serves a particular purpose.

---

## 2.1 Accounting as an information system

Accounting can be viewed as a system that converts **raw financial transactions into useful information**.

For example:

**Individual transactions**

↓

Purchase ₹50,000 of inventory
Sell goods for ₹80,000
Pay salary ₹10,000
Pay rent ₹5,000

↓

**Accounting records**

↓

Ledger balances and trial balance

↓

**Financial statements**

↓

Profit, assets, liabilities, cash flows, etc.

↓

**Decision-making**

Management, investors, lenders and other users can then evaluate the business.

---

# 3. Objectives / Purpose of Accounting

The broad purpose of accounting is to provide **reliable financial information for decision-making**.

More specifically, accounting helps to:

### 3.1 Determine profit or loss

Accounting allows a business to determine whether it has earned a **profit** or incurred a **loss** during a particular period.

In simplified form:

$$
\text{Profit} = \text{Revenue} - \text{Expenses}
$$

when revenue exceeds expenses.

If expenses exceed revenue, the result is a loss.

---

### 3.2 Determine financial position

Accounting helps determine what the business **owns** and what it **owes**.

This includes information about:

* Assets
* Liabilities
* Owner's equity/capital

The basic accounting relationship is:

$$
\boxed{\text{Assets}=\text{Liabilities}+\text{Equity}}
$$

This relationship becomes one of the most important foundations for everything that follows.

---

### 3.3 Track inventory / stock

Accounting helps the business determine:

* how much inventory it possesses,
* how much inventory has been purchased,
* how much has been sold,
* and, depending on the accounting system, the value of closing inventory.

This is particularly important for businesses dealing with physical goods.

---

### 3.4 Track amounts owed by and to the business

Accounting provides information about:

**Amounts receivable:**
Money customers or other parties owe the business.

**Amounts payable:**
Money the business owes suppliers, lenders, employees, governments, or other parties.

---

### 3.5 Support decision-making

Accounting information is used by different users to make economic decisions.

For example:

* Managers use it to plan and control operations.
* Investors use it to assess performance and prospects.
* Lenders use it to assess repayment ability.
* Owners use it to evaluate profitability.
* Other stakeholders use it to understand the financial condition of the business.

Therefore, accounting is not merely a historical record-keeping exercise. It is also a **decision-support system**.

---

# 4. Father of Accounting — Luca Pacioli

**Luca Pacioli** is commonly known as the **Father of Accounting**, particularly because of his important role in documenting and disseminating the principles of **double-entry bookkeeping**.

He was an Italian mathematician and Franciscan friar.

In **1494**, Pacioli published *Summa de Arithmetica*, which included a description of the Venetian method of bookkeeping based on double-entry principles.

### Why is Pacioli important?

His work helped formalize and spread systematic bookkeeping practices that became foundational to modern accounting.

### Important distinction

Pacioli did **not invent every underlying bookkeeping practice from nothing**. Rather, he is especially remembered for **documenting and popularizing the double-entry bookkeeping system**.

---

# 5. Functions of Accounting

The lecture identifies **seven major functions/processes of accounting**:

1. Identifying
2. Recording
3. Classifying
4. Summarising
5. Analysing
6. Interpreting
7. Communicating

These can be understood as stages through which raw business transactions are converted into useful financial information.

---

# 5.1 Identifying

### Meaning

The first function of accounting is to **identify business transactions of a financial character**.

The accountant determines which events:

1. relate to the business, and
2. have a financial effect that can be measured in monetary terms.

### Source documents

Transactions are identified using documents such as:

* Invoices
* Agreements/contracts
* Cash memos
* Receipts
* Bills
* Bank documents
* Purchase orders and other supporting records

### Example

Suppose a business purchases machinery for ₹2,00,000.

The invoice or other supporting documentation provides evidence of the transaction.

The accountant identifies that:

* the business acquired an asset,
* the transaction has a monetary value of ₹2,00,000,
* therefore it needs to be recorded.

### Key idea

> **Identification answers: "What financial transaction has occurred?"**

---

# 5.2 Recording

Once a transaction has been identified, it must be **systematically recorded**.

Transactions are generally recorded in **chronological order**, meaning according to the sequence in which they occur.

The primary record for this purpose is the **journal** (along with subsidiary/subsidiary books where applicable).

### Example

Suppose:

* January 1: Owner invests ₹1,00,000.
* January 3: Goods purchased for ₹30,000.
* January 5: Goods sold for ₹20,000.

The transactions are recorded in the appropriate order.

### Double-entry principle

Under double-entry bookkeeping, a transaction is recorded through corresponding **debits and credits**.

For every journal entry:

$$
\boxed{\text{Total Debits}=\text{Total Credits}}
$$

This equality is essential to maintaining the accounting equation and provides an internal check on the recording process.

### Key idea

> **Recording answers: "When and how should the transaction be entered into the accounting records?"**

---

# 5.3 Classifying

Merely recording transactions chronologically is not enough.

After transactions have been recorded, similar transactions need to be **grouped together**.

This process is called **classification**.

The grouping takes place through **ledger accounts**.

### Example

Suppose during the year the business makes:

* 50 cash sales
* 20 credit sales
* 15 purchases
* 12 salary payments

Instead of examining all individual transactions every time, the related transactions are accumulated in appropriate accounts such as:

* Sales Account
* Purchases Account
* Cash Account
* Salary Account
* Debtors/Receivables Account
* Creditors/Payables Account

Thus, the ledger organizes transactions account-wise.

### Why classification is necessary

It allows us to determine the total effect relating to each type of account.

For example, instead of searching through hundreds of transactions to determine total salary expense, we can look at the **Salary Account**.

### Trial Balance

After ledger accounts are prepared, a **trial balance** can be prepared to check the arithmetic equality of debit and credit balances.

### Key idea

> **Classification answers: "Which transactions belong together?"**

---

# 5.4 Summarising

Accounting records can contain hundreds, thousands, or even millions of individual transactions.

Decision-makers generally do not want to inspect every transaction individually.

Therefore, accounting information must be **summarised into meaningful statements**.

The classified information from the ledger/trial balance is used to prepare financial statements such as:

* **Income Statement / Profit and Loss Account**
* **Balance Sheet / Statement of Financial Position**
* **Cash Flow Statement**

These statements present large amounts of transaction data in a compact and useful form.

### Example

Instead of listing every salary payment separately, the financial statements may show:

> **Salary Expense = ₹6,50,000**

Instead of listing each asset purchase individually, the balance sheet presents the relevant asset balances.

### Key idea

> **Summarising answers: "What does all the recorded information amount to?"**

---

# 5.5 Analysing

After information has been summarised, it needs to be **analysed**.

Analysis involves examining relationships among financial statement items to understand the financial performance and position of the business.

According to the lecture, analysis helps identify the:

* **financial strengths** of the business, and
* **financial weaknesses** of the business.

### Examples

Suppose a company's:

* sales are increasing,
* profit margin is falling,
* debt is increasing,
* cash flow is deteriorating.

Simply looking at one number may not reveal the entire picture.

Analysis establishes relationships between items and helps us understand what those numbers imply.

This is particularly important in **financial analysis**, where techniques such as ratio analysis, trend analysis, and comparative analysis are used.

### Key idea

> **Analysis answers: "What relationships and patterns exist in the financial information?"**

---

# 5.6 Interpreting

Analysis produces relationships and patterns, but those relationships must then be given **meaning**.

This is the function of **interpretation**.

### Example

Suppose analysis shows that:

$$
\text{Debt-to-Equity Ratio}
$$

has increased significantly.

The interpretation might be that the company is becoming more dependent on debt financing, potentially increasing financial risk.

Thus:

**Analysis:** identifies the numerical relationship.
**Interpretation:** explains what that relationship means.

### Key idea

> **Interpretation answers: "What do the results of the analysis actually mean?"**

---

# 5.7 Communicating

The final function is to **communicate the accounting information to interested parties**.

The results obtained from accounting, after being:

* summarised,
* analysed, and
* interpreted,

are communicated to relevant users.

### Users may include

**Internal users**

* Management
* Owners
* Internal decision-makers

**External users**

* Investors/shareholders
* Banks and lenders
* Creditors
* Government/regulatory authorities
* Other interested stakeholders

The information is communicated primarily through financial reports and statements, along with other relevant reports.

### Key idea

> **Communication answers: "Who needs this information, and how should it be provided to them?"**

---

# 6. The Complete Accounting Process

The seven functions can be viewed as one continuous information-processing system:

$$
\boxed{
\text{Identifying}
\rightarrow
\text{Recording}
\rightarrow
\text{Classifying}
\rightarrow
\text{Summarising}
\rightarrow
\text{Analysing}
\rightarrow
\text{Interpreting}
\rightarrow
\text{Communicating}
}
$$

### Easy way to remember

**Identify → Record → Group → Summarise → Analyse → Explain → Communicate**

The important idea is that accounting starts with **raw financial events** and ends with **useful information for decision-making**.

---

# 7. Accounting Cycle

## 7.1 Meaning

The **accounting cycle** is the systematic process followed to record and process a company's financial transactions, beginning when a transaction occurs and ultimately resulting in the preparation of financial statements and closing of the accounts.

It is called a **cycle** because the process is repeated for each accounting period.

A simplified version presented in the lecture is:

$$
\boxed{
\text{Financial Transaction}
\rightarrow
\text{General Journal}
\rightarrow
\text{General Ledger}
\rightarrow
\text{Trial Balance}
\rightarrow
\text{Financial Statements}
}
$$

The cycle then starts again in the next accounting period.

---

# 8. Stages of the Accounting Cycle

## Stage 1 — Financial Transactions

The accounting cycle begins when a **financial transaction occurs**.

Examples:

* Purchase of goods
* Sale of goods
* Payment of expenses
* Receipt of cash
* Purchase of an asset
* Borrowing money
* Repayment of debt

The transaction must first be identified and measured in monetary terms.

### Example

A business purchases equipment for ₹1,00,000.

This is a financial transaction because:

* it relates to the business,
* it has financial consequences,
* it can be measured in money.

---

# Stage 2 — General Journal

Once identified, the transaction is recorded in the **journal**.

The journal records transactions in **chronological order**.

A journal entry normally specifies:

* the accounts affected,
* the debit(s),
* the credit(s),
* the amount,
* and an explanation/narration where required.

### Example

Business purchases equipment for cash ₹1,00,000:

| Account       |     Debit |    Credit |
| ------------- | --------: | --------: |
| Equipment A/c | ₹1,00,000 |         — |
| Cash A/c      |         — | ₹1,00,000 |

The fundamental requirement is:

$$
\boxed{\text{Debit} = \text{Credit}}
$$

---

# Stage 3 — Posting to the General Ledger

Journal entries are then **posted to the General Ledger (GL)**.

### What is a ledger?

A ledger contains individual accounts in which transactions relating to the same account are accumulated.

For example:

All transactions affecting **Cash** are brought together in the Cash Account.

All transactions affecting **Equipment** are brought together in the Equipment Account.

Thus, the ledger transforms the chronological information in the journal into an **account-wise classification**.

### Journal vs Ledger

| Journal                              | Ledger                                                        |
| ------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Records transactions chronologically | Groups transactions account-wise                              |
| First formal recording               | Classification of recorded transactions                       |
| Answers "when did it occur?"         | Helps determine "what is the balance/effect of this account?" |

---

# Stage 4 — Trial Balance

At the end of an accounting period, the balances of ledger accounts are used to prepare a **trial balance**.

The basic purpose highlighted in the lecture is to check the **arithmetical accuracy** of the accounting records.

A trial balance contains debit and credit balances.

Ideally:

$$
\boxed{\text{Total Debit Balances}=\text{Total Credit Balances}}
$$

### Important limitation

A trial balance agreeing does **not necessarily mean that every accounting error is absent**.

For example, certain errors can occur while still maintaining equal debits and credits.

Therefore, the trial balance is an important **check**, but it is not proof that the accounting records are completely error-free.

---

# Stage 5 — Financial Statements

After the necessary adjustments and finalisation of account balances, financial statements are prepared.

The major financial statements include:

### 1. Income Statement / Profit and Loss Account

Shows financial performance over a **period**.

It helps determine whether the business generated:

* Profit, or
* Loss.

---

### 2. Balance Sheet / Statement of Financial Position

Shows the financial position of the business at a **particular point in time**.

It presents:

$$
\boxed{\text{Assets}=\text{Liabilities}+\text{Equity}}
$$

---

### 3. Cash Flow Statement

Shows how cash and cash equivalents have changed during a period through different types of cash flows.

Broadly, cash flows are classified into:

* Operating activities
* Investing activities
* Financing activities

This statement is important because **profit and cash are not the same thing**.

---

# 9. Accounting Cycle vs. Functions of Accounting

These two concepts are closely related but should not be confused.

### Functions of accounting

Focus on **what accounting does**:

> Identify → Record → Classify → Summarise → Analyse → Interpret → Communicate

### Accounting cycle

Focuses on the **sequence of accounting procedures/processes**:

> Transaction → Journal → Ledger → Trial Balance → Financial Statements → Closing/next period

The accounting cycle therefore represents the operational flow by which accounting records are processed.

---

# 10. Big Picture: From Transaction to Decision

Consider a simple example.

A business:

* invests ₹5,00,000 capital,
* purchases inventory for ₹2,00,000,
* sells goods for ₹3,00,000,
* pays salary of ₹40,000,
* pays rent of ₹20,000.

These individual events are initially just **transactions**.

Accounting converts them into useful information:

**1. Identify**
Determine which events are financial transactions.

↓

**2. Record**
Enter them into the journal.

↓

**3. Classify**
Post them into appropriate ledger accounts.

↓

**4. Summarise**
Prepare trial balance and financial statements.

↓

**5. Analyse**
Examine profitability, liquidity, financial structure, etc.

↓

**6. Interpret**
Determine what the numbers indicate about the business.

↓

**7. Communicate**
Present the information to management, investors, lenders, and other users.

So the ultimate purpose is not merely:

> **"to maintain books."**

It is:

> **to convert financial transactions into meaningful information that supports judgement and decision-making.**

# 12. Important Conceptual Distinctions

### Accounting is not just bookkeeping

**Bookkeeping** primarily concerns the systematic recording and classification of transactions.

**Accounting** is broader. It includes:

> recording + classification + summarisation + analysis + interpretation + communication.

So:

$$
\boxed{\text{Bookkeeping is a part of accounting}}
$$

rather than the two being exactly the same thing.

---

### Profit is not the same as cash

A business can report a profit without having received all corresponding cash.

For example, a company may make a **credit sale**:

* Revenue is recognised according to applicable accounting principles.
* But cash may be received later.

Therefore:

$$
\boxed{\text{Profit} \neq \text{Cash}}
$$

This distinction will become extremely important when studying the **Income Statement and Cash Flow Statement**.

# 1. Accountancy

## Meaning of Accountancy

**Accountancy** refers to the **systematic body of knowledge concerning accounting**, including the **principles, concepts, rules, conventions, techniques and practices** that are applied in accounting.

In simple terms:

> **Accounting is the practical process of recording and processing financial information, whereas accountancy is the broader body of knowledge underlying that process.**

Accountancy therefore includes the theoretical and practical framework within which accounting is carried out.

### Relationship

$$
\boxed{\text{Accountancy} = \text{Theory + Principles + Concepts + Practices of Accounting}}
$$

---

# 2. Bookkeeping vs Accounting

A very important distinction is:

> **Bookkeeping is primarily concerned with recording and classifying financial transactions, while accounting is concerned with summarising, analysing, interpreting and communicating the resulting information.**

The lecture compares them on several bases.

| Basis                | Bookkeeping                                                          | Accounting                                                          |
| -------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Objective**        | To maintain systematic records of transactions of a financial nature | To ascertain the net results and financial position of the business |
| **Phase**            | Recording phase of the accounting system                             | Summarising phase and subsequent interpretation/analysis            |
| **Stage**            | Primary/initial stage                                                | Secondary stage, beginning after bookkeeping                        |
| **Nature of skills** | Routine and clerical in nature                                       | Analytical in nature; requires accounting knowledge and judgement   |
| **Who performs it?** | Bookkeepers / bookkeeping staff                                      | Accountants / accounting staff                                      |

---

## 2.1 Bookkeeping

Bookkeeping is the process of **recording business transactions having monetary aspects in the books of accounts**.

It primarily deals with:

* Identifying transactions
* Measuring transactions in monetary terms
* Recording transactions
* Classifying transactions

Thus:

$$
\boxed{\text{Bookkeeping} \rightarrow \text{Identify + Measure + Record + Classify}}
$$

### Nature of bookkeeping

Bookkeeping is largely:

* Routine
* Clerical
* Procedural
* Record-oriented

It generally requires less analytical judgement than the later stages of accounting.

---

## 2.2 Accounting

Accounting is broader than bookkeeping.

It begins with the information generated by bookkeeping and involves:

* Summarising
* Analysing
* Interpreting
* Communicating

Thus:

$$
\boxed{\text{Accounting} \rightarrow \text{Summarise + Analyse + Interpret + Communicate}}
$$

### Overall relationship

$$
\boxed{\text{Bookkeeping} \subset \text{Accounting}}
$$

Bookkeeping provides the **basic accounting records**, while accounting turns those records into **meaningful financial information**.

### Simple flow

$$
\text{Transactions}
\rightarrow
\boxed{\text{Bookkeeping}}
\rightarrow

\boxed{\text{Accounting}}

$$

# 3. Objectives of Accounting

The lecture identifies four major objectives.

---

## 3.1 Maintain a systematic record of business transactions

The first objective is to maintain a **complete and systematic record of business transactions** according to established accounting rules and procedures.

A complete record helps reduce:

* omission of transactions,
* loss of information,
* fraud or unauthorized manipulation,
* dependence on human memory.

### Process

Transactions are initially recorded in:

* Journal, or
* Subsidiary books,

and subsequently posted to the:

* Ledger.

Thus:

$$
\text{Transaction}
\rightarrow
\text{Journal/Subsidiary Books}
\rightarrow
\text{Ledger}
$$

### Why systematic recording matters

Imagine a business carrying out thousands of transactions every year. Relying on memory would make accurate determination of profit, assets, liabilities, receivables and expenses impossible.

Accounting creates a **permanent and organized record**.

---

# 3.2 Calculate profit or loss

The second major objective is to **ascertain the net profit earned or net loss suffered during a particular accounting period**.

The business's revenues and expenses are considered to determine its operating result.

In simplified form:

$$
\boxed{\text{Profit}=\text{Revenue}-\text{Expenses}}
$$

when revenue exceeds expenses.

If expenses exceed revenue:

$$
\boxed{\text{Loss}=\text{Expenses}-\text{Revenue}}
$$

### Financial statement used

The **Income Statement / Profit and Loss Account** is prepared to determine the result for the accounting period.

### Important point

Profit or loss relates to a **period of time**, such as:

* one month,
* one quarter,
* one financial year.

---

# 3.3 Ascertain the financial position of the business

Profit alone does not tell us the complete story about a business.

A business may have earned a profit but still have substantial liabilities or insufficient resources.

Therefore, accounting also aims to determine the **financial position** of the business.

This is presented through the:

$$
\boxed{\text{Balance Sheet / Statement of Financial Position}}
$$

The balance sheet provides information about the business's:

### Assets

Resources controlled by the business.

### Liabilities

Obligations owed by the business to outsiders.

### Capital / Equity

The owners' residual interest in the business.

The fundamental relationship is:

$$
\boxed{\text{Assets}=\text{Liabilities}+\text{Equity}}
$$

The lecture describes the balance sheet as a **"screen picture" of the financial position** because it provides a snapshot of the business's position at a particular point in time.

### Important distinction

**Income Statement:**

> What happened during the period?

**Balance Sheet:**

> What is the financial position at a particular date?

---

# 3.4 Provide information to various parties

Accounting also aims to **provide financial information to interested parties**.

These may include:

* Owners
* Management
* Creditors
* Banks and lenders
* Government authorities
* Investors
* Other stakeholders

This information allows users to make appropriate economic decisions.

Thus, accounting is ultimately an **information and decision-support function**.

# 4.1 Financial Accounting

## Meaning

Financial accounting is concerned with:

* systematically recording financial transactions,
* preparing financial reports,
* determining profit or loss,
* determining financial position,
* reporting sources and uses of cash.

Its broad purpose is to present information about an organization's **financial performance and financial position**.

### Main outputs

Financial accounting primarily results in financial statements such as:

* Income Statement
* Balance Sheet
* Cash Flow Statement

### What does it tell us?

Financial accounting helps determine:

**Performance**

$$
\text{Profit / Loss}
$$

**Position**

$$
\text{Assets, Liabilities, Equity}
$$

**Cash**

$$
\text{Cash Inflows and Outflows}
$$

### Key idea

> **Financial accounting is primarily concerned with reporting financial information about the business.**

---

# 4.2 Cost Accounting

## Meaning

Cost accounting focuses on the **analysis and determination of the cost** of products manufactured or services provided by a business.

It helps determine:

* Cost of production
* Cost of individual products/services
* Cost behaviour
* Cost control opportunities
* Efficiency of operations

### Example

Suppose a company manufactures a product.

Its costs may include:

* Raw materials
* Direct labour
* Factory overhead
* Power
* Transportation
* Other production expenses

Cost accounting helps determine the cost associated with producing the product.

### Management use

Cost accounting also helps in:

> **Controlling costs and providing necessary costing information to management for decision-making.**

Thus, it is closely connected with managerial decisions regarding cost efficiency and profitability.

---

# 4.3 Management Accounting

Management accounting uses relevant financial and cost information to support **internal management decisions**.

The lecture notes that management accounting draws relevant information mainly from:

* Financial accounting
* Cost accounting

It helps management in decisions such as:

### Budgeting

Planning future revenues, expenses, cash requirements, etc.

### Profitability assessment

Determining which products, activities, customers or divisions are more profitable.

### Pricing decisions

Determining or evaluating appropriate prices based on costs, demand and profitability considerations.

### Capital expenditure decisions

Evaluating major long-term investments, such as purchasing machinery or expanding facilities.

### Key idea

> **Management accounting converts accounting information into information useful for managerial planning, control and decision-making.**

---

# 5. Financial Accounting vs Cost Accounting vs Management Accounting

| Feature              | Financial Accounting                                    | Cost Accounting                | Management Accounting                                   |
| -------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------- |
| Main focus           | Overall financial performance and position              | Cost determination and control | Management decision-making                              |
| Main users           | Primarily external users, though also useful internally | Management                     | Internal management                                     |
| Information          | Financial transactions and statements                   | Product/service/process costs  | Financial + cost + other relevant information           |
| Major purpose        | Reporting                                               | Cost analysis and control      | Planning, control and decisions                         |
| Typical applications | Profit, assets, liabilities, cash flows                 | Product costing, cost control  | Budgeting, pricing, profitability, investment decisions |

### Relationship

A useful conceptual flow is:

$$
\boxed{
\text{Financial Accounting + Cost Accounting}
\rightarrow
\text{Management Accounting}
\rightarrow
\text{Management Decisions}
}
$$

Management accounting is therefore not completely independent of financial and cost accounting; it makes extensive use of information produced by them.

# 6. Accounting as Both a Science and an Art

A common theoretical question is:

> **Is accounting a science or an art?**

The lecture's answer is:

$$
\boxed{\text{Accounting is both a science and an art.}}
$$

---

# 6.1 Accounting as a Science

Science involves a **systematic body of knowledge** developed through observation, study, practice, investigation, rules and principles.

Accounting has many features associated with a science because it is based on:

* Principles
* Concepts
* Rules
* Conventions
* Standards
* Systematic procedures

Accounting follows an organized method for identifying, measuring, recording, classifying and reporting financial transactions.

Therefore:

> **Accounting is a science because it contains a systematic and organized body of knowledge governed by established principles and standards.**

---

# 6.2 Accounting as an Art

Art refers to the **application of knowledge, methods and techniques to practical situations**.

Accounting is an art because merely knowing accounting principles is not enough. Those principles must be **applied correctly to actual business situations**.

For example, an accountant must apply accounting rules to:

* Record transactions,
* Prepare accounts,
* Make necessary estimates,
* Present financial information,
* Produce meaningful financial statements.

Thus:

> **Accounting is an art because it involves the practical application of accounting principles, methods and techniques.**

### Combined view

$$
\boxed{
\text{Science}=\text{Knowledge and principles}
}
$$

$$
\boxed{
\text{Art}=\text{Practical application of that knowledge}
}
$$

Hence:

$$
\boxed{\text{Accounting = Science + Art}}
$$

# 7. Uses / Advantages of Accounting

Accounting provides several practical benefits to a business.

The lecture highlights the following.

---

## 7.1 Protecting business assets

A systematic accounting system provides records of:

* cash,
* inventory,
* machinery,
* receivables,
* other assets.

Proper records make it easier to monitor assets and detect irregularities.

Thus accounting helps in the **control and protection of business assets**.

## 7.2 Facilitates settlement of tax liabilities

Systematic accounting records provide evidence of business transactions and financial results.

They help in:

* calculating tax liabilities,
* preparing tax-related information,
* supporting the correctness of reported transactions.

Proper accounting records can therefore facilitate compliance with tax obligations.

## 7.3 Replaces memory

A business may conduct a huge number of transactions.

It is impossible to remember:

* every sale,
* every purchase,
* every payment,
* every receipt,

Accounting provides a **permanent systematic record**, eliminating dependence on memory.

Therefore:

$$
\boxed{\text{Accounting records replace reliance on human memory}}
$$

---

## 7.4 Facilitates comparative study

Accounting records make it possible to compare the business's performance across periods.

For example:

$$
\text{Profit in 2025}

$$

$$
\text{Sales growth}
$$

$$
\text{Expense growth}

\text{Profit margin changes}

Comparisons help management identify significant changes and investigate the factors responsible for them.

## 7.5 Facilitates loans

Banks and financial institutions often need financial information before granting loans.

Accounting records can provide evidence relating to:

* business performance,

## 7.6 Facilitates sale of business

# 8. Limitations of Accounting

Accounting is extremely useful, but it is **not a perfect representation of everything happening in a business**.

The lecture highlights several limitations.

---

# 8.1 Accounting ignores qualitative elements

Accounting is primarily concerned with matters that can be **measured in monetary terms**.

Therefore, important qualitative factors may not be directly reflected in the accounting records.

Examples include:

* Quality of employees
* Employee morale
* Industrial relations
* Quality of management
* Public relations
* Brand reputation
* Customer loyalty

### Example

Two companies may have identical financial statements, yet one may have a much stronger brand and more loyal customers.

Traditional financial accounting may not capture this difference adequately.

Therefore:

> **Accounting provides quantitative financial information, but important qualitative factors may remain outside the accounting records.**

---

# 8.2 Accounting may lead to window dressing

## Meaning of window dressing

**Window dressing** refers to the presentation or manipulation of financial accounts in a way that makes the business appear financially stronger or better than its actual condition.

The purpose can be to:

* conceal unfavourable facts,
* improve the apparent financial position,
* present a more attractive picture to users.

### Example

Management might use accounting choices or timing of transactions to make year-end numbers appear more favourable, subject to what is permitted or prohibited by accounting standards and law.

### Important distinction

Window dressing is not an inherent purpose of accounting.

Rather:

> **It is a potential misuse or manipulation of accounting information.**

---

# 8.3 Accounting is often based on historical cost

Traditional accounting frequently records assets using their **historical cost**, i.e., the amount originally paid to acquire them, subject to the applicable accounting framework and subsequent adjustments.

### Problem

Historical cost may become less representative over time because of:

* Inflation
* Changes in market prices
* Changes in purchasing power
* Changes in economic conditions

### Example

Suppose land was purchased decades ago for ₹10 lakh and is now worth ₹2 crore.

A historical-cost-based figure may not fully reflect its current economic value.

Therefore:

> **Historical-cost measurement may reduce the relevance of accounting information when current economic values differ substantially from historical amounts.**

---

# 8.4 Accounting is not fully exact

Many accounting figures are based on **estimates and assumptions**, rather than being directly observable with complete certainty.

Examples include estimating:

* Useful life of an asset
* Depreciation
* Provision for doubtful/bad debts
* Expected losses
* Certain inventory values or recoverability
* Other provisions and valuation adjustments

### Example: depreciation

A machine's actual useful life cannot always be known precisely when it is purchased.

An estimate must be made regarding how long it will be useful.

Thus:

> **Accounting contains estimates and professional judgement, so not every reported figure is perfectly exact.**

### Important distinction

This does **not** mean accounting is arbitrary.

Accounting estimates are generally made using:

* established principles,
* standards,
* available evidence,
* assumptions,
* professional judgement.

---

# 9. Summary of Accounting Limitations

| Limitation                      | Meaning                                                                                |
| ------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Qualitative factors ignored** | Factors such as employee quality, reputation and morale may not be adequately captured |
| **Window dressing**             | Accounts may potentially be presented/manipulated to show a better financial picture   |
| **Historical cost**             | Historical amounts may become less representative of current values                    |
| **Not fully exact**             | Some figures depend on estimates and judgement                                         |

### Central idea

Financial statements should therefore be **interpreted carefully**. They are highly useful, but they do not provide a complete picture of every economic aspect of a business.

The lecture classifies accounting information into three broad categories:

$$
\boxed{
\text{Profit/Surplus Information}
}
$$

$$
\boxed{
\text{Financial Position Information}
}
$$

$$
\boxed{
\text{Cash Flow Information}
}
$$

---

# 10.1 Information relating to Profit or Surplus

The **Income Statement / Profit and Loss Account** provides information about the:

* profit earned, or
* loss incurred

during an accounting period.

It reflects the results arising from business operations and other relevant items according to the applicable accounting framework.

### Key question answered

> **Did the business make a profit or a loss during the period?**

### Time perspective

It is a **period-based statement**.

For example:

> Profit for the year ended 31 March 2026.

---

# 10.2 Information relating to Financial Position

The **Balance Sheet / Statement of Financial Position** provides information about the financial position of an entity at a particular date.

It presents information about:

* Assets
* Liabilities
* Equity/Capital

### Key question answered

> **What does the business own and what does it owe at a particular date?**

### Time perspective

It is a **point-in-time statement**.

For example:

> Financial position as at 31 March 2026.

---

## Special point for not-for-profit organisations

For a not-for-profit organisation, the difference between assets and liabilities is generally referred to as the **General Fund** rather than owner's capital/equity in the traditional profit-oriented sense.

Conceptually:

$$
\text{Net Assets}=\text{Assets}-\text{Liabilities}
$$

and for such entities this may be represented by the General Fund, subject to the relevant accounting framework.

---

# 10.3 Information about Cash Flow

The **Cash Flow Statement** shows the movement of cash during a specific accounting period.

It reports:

* **Cash inflows**
* **Cash outflows**

and helps explain changes in cash and cash equivalents.

### Why is cash-flow information important?

Many business decisions depend on the availability of cash.

For example:

* Paying liabilities
* Paying dividends
* Purchasing assets
* Expanding operations
* Meeting short-term obligations

A business can report accounting profit while still experiencing cash-flow problems.

Therefore:

$$
\boxed{\text{Profitability and cash availability are different concepts}}
$$

---

# 11. The Three Major Financial Information Questions

A very useful way to remember the three types of information is:

| Question                                          | Information                 | Statement              |
| ------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------- | ---------------------- |
| **How did the business perform?**                 | Profit/Loss                 | Income Statement / P&L |
| **What is the business's position?**              | Assets, liabilities, equity | Balance Sheet          |
| **Where did cash come from and where did it go?** | Cash inflows/outflows       | Cash Flow Statement    |

Together, these statements provide a much more complete understanding of a business.
